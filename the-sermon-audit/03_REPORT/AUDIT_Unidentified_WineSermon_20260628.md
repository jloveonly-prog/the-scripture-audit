#### 📖 Webster's 1828 Dictionary, "WINE" Entry — the Actual Original Text

Webster's 1828 American Dictionary of the English Language's actual definition of WINE reads as follows:

> **WINE**, *n.* [Sax. *win*; G. *wein*; D. *wijn*; Sw. and Dan. *vin*; Fr. *vin*; It. and Sp. *vino*; Port. *vinho*; L. *vinum*; Ir. *fin* or *fion*; W. *gwin*; Arm. *guin*; Russ. *vino*.]
>
> **1.** The **fermented** juice of grapes; as the wine of Madeira; the wine of Burgundy or of Tokay.
>
> **2.** The juice of certain fruits, prepared with sugar, spirits, etc.; as currant wine; gooseberry wine.
>
> **3.** Intoxication. Noah awoke from his wine. Gen. 9.
>
> **4.** Drinking. They that tarry long at the wine. Prov. 23.
>
> **Wine** is of several sorts: **red**, **white**, or **amber-colored** or **reddish-white**...
>
> New wine, wine pressed from the grape but not fermented.

#### 🔍 Precision Analysis — the Preacher's Claim vs. the Actual Dictionary

| The Preacher's Claim | The Dictionary's Actual Text | Verdict |
|:---|:---|:---:|
| "the first meaning of wine is grape juice" | **Definition 1**: "The **fermented** juice of grapes" — the **fermented** juice of grapes | ❌ False |
| "grape juice is the basic word, regardless of fermentation" | Definition 1 explicitly includes "fermented," and this is the **first and basic definition** | ❌ False |
| "new grape juice has not fermented" | the dictionary has a separate "New wine" entry at the very end: "wine pressed from the grape **but not fermented**" — the qualifier "but not fermented" had to be attached precisely because **the base word "wine" itself is fermented**, requiring "not fermented" to distinguish it | ✅ only "new wine" is separately marked |

#### 📖 Cross-Verification with Samuel Johnson's English Dictionary (1755)

A definition from roughly 140 years after the KJV translation (1611):

> **WINE**: "The **fermented** juice of the grape."
> "...no fruit, taken crude, has the **intoxicating** quality of wine."

#### 📖 Additional Verification with Merriam-Webster (Modern)

> **WINE**: "1a: **fermented** grape juice containing varying percentages of alcohol together with ethers and esters that give it bouquet and flavor"
> "1b: **wine or a substitute** used in Christian communion services"

Even in the modern Merriam-Webster dictionary, definition 1 is **"fermented grape juice."**

#### 📖 Additional Verification with the Oxford English Dictionary (OED)

> **WINE**: "An alcoholic drink made from **fermented** grape juice."

#### ⚖️ Comprehensive Ruling

| Dictionary | Year Published | "Wine" Definition 1 | "Fermented" Included? |
|:---:|:---:|:---|:---:|
| Samuel Johnson | 1755 | "The fermented juice of the grape" | ✅ |
| Webster's 1828 | 1828 | "The fermented juice of grapes" | ✅ |
| Merriam-Webster (Modern) | Modern | "fermented grape juice containing varying percentages of alcohol" | ✅ |
| Oxford OED | Modern | "An alcoholic drink made from fermented grape juice" | ✅ |

**Without exception, all 4 dictionaries explicitly specify "fermented" in wine's first (basic) definition.**

**Then does Webster's 1828 carry a nuance encompassing 'grape beverages' in general?**

> Yes — but in an entirely different manner from what the preacher claimed.

Webster's 1828 WINE entry is structured to list derivative usages after the basic definition 1 ("fermented grape juice"):
- **Definition 2**: a beverage made from other fruits (currant, gooseberry) can also be called wine (the concept of fruit wine)
- **Definition 3**: a metonymic usage calling "intoxication" itself wine (Noah "awoke from his wine" — Gen 9)
- **Definition 4**: a metonymic usage calling "the act of tarrying long in drinking" itself wine (Prov 23)
- **New wine**: marked as a **separate entry** at the very end of the dictionary — "pressed from the grape but **not fermented**"

That is, **Webster's 1828 Dictionary does show that the word "wine" is a broad word that, in a wide sense, can encompass grape-related beverages generally.** But its core meaning (definition 1) is unmistakably **"fermented" grape juice**, and the dictionary's own structure requires that anything unfermented always carry the **separate qualifier "New wine"** to distinguish it.

**The preacher's errors are as follows:**
1. **deleted the core word "fermented"** from the dictionary's definition 1, quoting only "grape juice"
2. mixed the separate entry "New wine (unfermented)" in as if it were the basic definition
3. drew, on this basis, the doctrinal conclusion that "wine = grape juice = non-alcoholic by default"

> **This is a dictionary distortion falling under TYPE-T (lexical shift/selective citation).**

## 5. Spiritual Lesson (LESSON-6)

Three lessons can be drawn from this sermon audit:

**First, distorting Scripture to draw a good conclusion is not biblical.**
Warning of the dangers of alcohol is unquestionably necessary and good. But to make that warning, selectively citing a dictionary's definition, deleting a biblical verse's qualifying conditions, and omitting contrary evidence is a dangerous method of "defending the truth with something that is not the truth." The Word of God is powerful enough as it is — we do not need to prop it up through distortion.

**Second, distinguish between "drunkenness" and "the substance."**
What Scripture condemns is **not the substance wine, but the act of drunkenness.** Losing this distinction leads to the logical dead end of "Jesus committed sin (since He made and drank wine)." Temperance regarding the substance is a 19th-century social movement, not the teaching of the 1st-century biblical text.

**Third, a dictionary citation must be accurate.**
Citing Webster's 1828 Dictionary's definition of "wine" while deleting the core word "fermented" — whether intentional or a mistake — results in misleading the audience. When a preacher cites an authoritative source, the audience trusts that the citation is accurate. Guarding that trust is a preacher's most basic duty.
