• It's possible. But the "one-shot" answer depends on whether the opponent holds Scheme A or Scheme B.

  The strongest counters are the three that work against Scheme B as well.

  1. "If the name 'Father' only came into being after the Incarnation, how do you read Isaiah 63:16's 'thy name is from everlasting'?"

  2. "1 John 4:14 says 'the Father sent the Son.' How do you explain a sentence in which the Father sent the Son — a title you say only arose at the Incarnation — *before* that Incarnation?"

  3. "In 1 Corinthians 15:24, the 'he' who delivers up the kingdom to the Father is said to be the Father — yet verses 27-28 distinguish 'the Son himself' from the one who put all things under him. Why does the pronoun's referent shift within the same paragraph?"

  If the opponent uses Scheme A — the scheme in which body=Jesus, soul=the Father, spirit=the Holy Spirit — the following are additionally strong.

  - the baptism scene: Jesus on earth, the Spirit descending upon Him, and a voice heard from heaven.
  - Matt 26:39: the distinction between "my will" and "thy will."
  - Gen 2:7: the reversal that occurs when you try to fit the sentence structure of body/breath/soul into the scheme.

  Conversely, do not use as an opening attack: the application of Ps 2:7's resurrection language in Acts 13:33, the flat assertion that "being sent necessarily implies pre-existence," or a philosophical statement like "one cannot deliver a kingdom up to oneself." These make it too easy for the opponent to simply shift the point of contention.

  In practice, it is best to ask this first:

  > "Does Jesus have one soul, or two — a human soul and a divine soul? And is the Father right now in heaven, or within Christ?"

  Once you have the answer, deploy only the single card that fits that scheme. Throwing several arguments at once lets the opponent seize on only the weakest one and evade.




  It's possible. The structure is good, and branching into Scheme A and Scheme B is a great strength in the field. But as the draft currently stands, the opponent could seize on a few points to shake the credibility of the whole. My assessment: 6/10 before revision, 8/10 if you fix the following.

  The first thing to fix is the "right hand" statistic. A full survey of the KJV shows that, of the 14 instances:

  - explicitly "sitting/seated": 9 instances
  - "standing": Acts 7:55-56, 2 instances
  - posture not stated: Acts 2:33, Rom 8:34, 1 Pet 3:22, 3 instances

  So the sentence "in twelve places He is seated" does not match the facts. Change it to this:

  > "Of the fourteen instances, nine directly state that He is seated, two directly state that He is standing, and three do not state a posture at all. The only place where 'standing' is directly recorded is the two verses concerning Stephen."

  And in the Stephen passage, it would be better to tone down the expressions "three appear in one sentence" and "two persons are seen, each in a different location." The text speaks of the glory of God, Jesus, and the right hand of God marking Jesus's position. Saying "three visible objects" allows the counter-rebuttal that "glory is not an independent person." This much is enough for the core point:

  > "Stephen records, across two consecutive verses, that he saw the glory of God and saw Jesus standing at the right hand of God. Any explanation claiming 'Jesus is that very Person Himself' must adequately account for the distinct referents in the sentence."

  Also, in a few places, converting from an attack into a question form greatly improves the defensive strength.

  - "For someone to be sent, the son must already exist beforehand"
    → "In 1 John 4:14's sentence, 'the Father sent the Son,' how do you read the explanation that this is a title that only arose at the Incarnation?"
    Rather than immediately forcing pre-existence, this shifts the burden of explanation onto the opponent.

  - "Having a will means being a person"
    → "In Matthew 26:39, whose will is 'my will,' and whose is 'thy will'? If the two wills are in fact distinct, what is the textual basis for reading this as one person's internal dialogue with himself?"
    This avoids the philosophical debate of "will = personhood."

  - "Jesus saw the Father"
    → soften this using John 6:46: "in context, He is referring to Himself, saying that only the One who is of God has seen the Father." The sentence itself is not first-person.

  Keep the good counters as they are.

  - Isa 63:16: the text where the name "Father" is "from everlasting."
  - 1 Cor 15:24, 27-28: the sentence's distinction between the one who delivers the kingdom to the Father, the Son himself, and the One who subjects all things.
  - John 8:13, 17-18: the structure in which, facing the objection of self-testimony, Jesus presents "the testimony of two men" together with the Father.
  - Acts 13:2: the Holy Spirit's direct first-person utterance, "I / unto me."
  - Luke 3:21-22: the structure in which Jesus, the descent of the Spirit, and the voice from heaven are juxtaposed within the same event.
  - Isa 63:16 and 1 Cor 15 for Scheme B. The current design — not throwing the Scheme-A cards (Gen 2:7, Gethsemane) at Scheme B — is also good.

  The final conclusion, too, is stronger phrased like this rather than "solid proof supporting the Trinity":

  > "These texts speak of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit as distinct from one another, while declaring that God is one. Both of these lines must be read without erasing either one."

  Done this way, you can precisely strike the textual gaps in the opponent's scheme without using a specific systematic label as the grounds for your conclusion.
