## 1. The Number of Souls / Gethsemane

**The Doctrine's Claim**: the soul is the Father, the body is Jesus, the spirit is the Holy Spirit.

**Biblical Rebuttal**: in Matt 26:38, Jesus says, "**my soul** is exceeding sorrowful, unto death." Substituting the soul=Father scheme as-is means the One grieving at this moment must be the Father — a conclusion that results in exactly **the direction this doctrine declares it rejects** (the conclusion that the Father Himself directly suffers). The same scene's "not as **I** will, but as **thou** wilt" (Matt 26:39) is a dialogue structure split between 1st and 2nd person, differing even from the way Scripture describes actual internal conflict (Rom 7:15-23, 1st-person singular). Luke 22:44's sweat like drops of blood is a physiological response manifested in the body, and cannot be dismissed with "only the Father was in anguish." If one answers, "Jesus also has a separate soul," then the soul=Father label collapses, and 1 Thess 5:23's spirit-soul-body analogy (within a person, the body does not separately possess its own soul) breaks as well. Answering, "'soul' is just an idiomatic expression referring to 'myself,' not a real, distinct term," also fails to escape — because the instant "soul" is reduced to an idiom, the foundational label "soul=Father" itself becomes void (an idiom cannot serve as a label designating a specific person). Answering with the soliloquy analogy that "even a Christian speaks to himself" (Eph 5:19, "speaking to yourselves") also fails to escape — Eph 5:19's "to yourselves" (ἑαυτοῖς) is a mutual-reciprocal expression in which the plural congregation sings **to one another** (the same construction as Col 3:16's "teaching one another"), and does not refer to an individual's monologue. Isaiah 53:10-12 mentions "his soul" of the Messiah three times, showing that this problem is established not as a special New Testament case but already at the Old Testament prophetic stage. John 12:27 ("now is my soul troubled") reconfirms the same structure. Acts 2:27, 31 (Peter citing David's Ps 16:10) reconfirms this problem from another angle — "**thou** wilt not leave my soul in hell" (2nd-person subject + 1st-person possessive "my soul"), followed by "**his** soul was not left in hell" (a shift to 3rd person). Substituting the "soul=Father" scheme turns verse 27 into "thou wilt not leave thy own soul," making the 2nd-person subject and the 1st-person possessive the same referent, and voiding the petitionary construction itself of meaning. Two authors — David (in the Psalms) and Peter (in Acts) — both use this distinction as a premise of their argument.

**Verdict**: ❌ UNBIBLICAL — IRONCLAD/STRONG (Matt 26:38-39, Luke 22:44, 1 Thess 5:23, Isa 53:10-12, Acts 2:27, 31, converging)

> 🚨 **This rebuttal does not directly apply to Version B of the scheme (see §9-4, required reading).** The core driver of the rebuttal above is Version A's premise that **"Jesus has only one soul."** It is only when there is one soul that the substitution "that soul = the Father" is forced. **Version B answers that Jesus has two souls (God's soul + a human soul), and identifies Matt 26:38's "my soul" specifically as the human one.** Then the substitution above never fires in the first place.
> → for a Version-B opponent, use **the 3 rebuttals of §9-4-3 (⑴~⑶)** instead of this item. If the opponent counter-attacks, meet it with **⑷⑸ (prepared for anticipated counter-rebuttals)** in the same section. See §9-4-2 for how to tell Version A from Version B.
>
> 🚫 **Do not attempt to rebut Version B's "the soul is not annihilated" (see §12, §13-7).** That statement is **biblically true**, and attempting to rebut it invites a counter-attack.

> **Note**: there is also an argument that uses this issue in the opposite direction — "since the Father and the Son each have a soul, there were, from the start, two Gods." This reaches a conclusion diametrically opposite the Godhead doctrine while sharing the same premise — "the number of souls = the number of beings" — and that premise itself is an extension incompatible with the "one God" that Deut 6:4, Isa 44:6, and James 2:19 consistently proclaim (⚠️ UNSUPPORTED).

## 2. The Legal-Witness Structure

**The Doctrine's Claim**: 1 John 5:7, "there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost, and these three are one," describes a single person testifying to Himself, meaning only "the agreement of testimony."

**Biblical Rebuttal**: John 8:17-18 is not an isolated statement but an answer to the objection raised in the immediately preceding verse 13 — "the Pharisees said unto him, **Thou bearest record of thyself; thy record is not true**" (John 8:13). This objection is precisely that "self-testimony is invalid," and for Jesus's answer in verses 17-18 to rebut that objection, He must present an independent second witness other than Himself. His statement, "**I** am one that bear witness of myself, and **the Father that sent me** beareth witness of me," is, grammatically, a two-witness structure with subjects distinctly split between "I" and "the Father that sent me" — unless the sender and the one sent are separate referents, the transitive construction "sent me" itself does not hold. What Deut 19:15 ("at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses") counts is **not the number of times testimony is given, but the number of witnesses.** Even if one person speaks twice, in two different places, that remains legally a single witness — so the explanation "one person testifying separately from earth and from heaven" does not satisfy the personnel requirement Deut 19:15 demands. Note that "an oath" (Heb 6:13, God swearing by Himself) and "testimony" (Deut 19:15) are separate institutions with different purposes and requirements, so Heb 6:13 does not answer this point.

**Verdict**: ❌ UNBIBLICAL — IRONCLAD/STRONG (John 8:13, 17-18, Deut 19:15, Matt 18:16)

## 3. The Reversal of the Gen 2:7 Scheme

**The Doctrine's Claim**: the scheme in which body=Jesus and spirit=Holy Spirit combine to become the soul (=the Father), or the claim that "the breath of life" (Gen 2:7) is itself the Holy Spirit, and the Holy Spirit is the same as the Father's breath.

**Biblical Rebuttal**: Gen 2:7, "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul," is, by sentence structure, **[body (dust) + spirit (breath of life)] → [soul].** Substituting the Godhead doctrine's scheme (soul=Father / body=Jesus / spirit=Holy Spirit) as-is produces the exact opposite conclusion — that the Father is "the resulting product of combining Jesus and the Holy Spirit."

**Verdict**: ❌ UNBIBLICAL — STRONG (Gen 2:7, sentence structure)

> 🚨 **The Object of Substitution Changes for Version B of the Scheme (see §9-4).** This rebuttal holds only when substituted into Version A's **1:1 label assignment** — "soul=Father / body=Jesus / spirit=Holy Spirit." Version B relabels as "deity=Father / humanity=Son," so it does not directly overlay onto the three terms of Gen 2:7.
> → **However, it does not die entirely.** As long as Version B also maintains the tripartite statement that "God is three who are God, and one of those three is the body," substituting those three into Gen 2:7 produces the same reversal the moment it is done. **When using this card against a Version-B opponent, first confirm, "do you hold that God consists of three elements?"** — throwing it without confirming invites the reply, "I never said that."

> **An Honest Supplementary Observation**: "the breath of life" is the Hebrew *neshamah* (נְשָׁמָה), not *ruach* (רוּחַ, spirit/Holy Spirit). However, since the two words are used in parallel apposition in Job 33:4, they cannot be declared entirely unrelated — this observation is used only as supplementary evidence, with the main argument resting on the sentence structure (the reversal).

## 4. 1 John 1:3's "Fellowship"

**The Doctrine's Claim**: prayer is "fellowship between God's soul and our soul."

**Biblical Rebuttal**: "fellowship" (κοινωνία) is, by definition, a word that holds only between two or more distinct parties — one does not speak of having fellowship with oneself. Yet 1 John 1:3 **lists the object of this "fellowship" as two — the Father and the Son —** and **repeats the Greek preposition μετά ("with") twice, once before each object.** Repeating a preposition this way in Greek is the standard grammar used to distinguish multiple objects (Rev 22:1's repeated article, τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ τοῦ ἀρνίου, belongs to the same family). Applying the premise "fellowship holds only between two distinct parties" directly to 1 John 1:3 amounts to acknowledging the distinction between the Father and the Son.

**Verdict**: ❌ UNBIBLICAL — STRONG (1 John 1:3, the repeated μετά grammar)

## 5. Three Simultaneous Points at the Baptism

**The Doctrine's Claim**: the heavenly voice and the dove in the baptism scene (Luke 3:21-22) are merely "the soul speaking to the body."

**Biblical Rebuttal**: this single scene contains three simultaneously and spatially distinct points — ① Jesus being baptized in the water (earth), ② the Spirit descending in the bodily form of a dove **upon** Jesus (ἐπ' αὐτόν — a preposition denoting something distinct from the object coming down upon it), and ③ a voice heard **from heaven.** The explanation "the soul spoke to the body" addresses only two points (soul↔body), and does not explain the simultaneous occurrence of all three. The parallel verses Mark 1:11 and Luke 3:22 use a 2nd-person vocative ("**Thou** art my beloved Son"), explicitly establishing a speaker↔addressee dialogue structure. Matt 3:17 describes the same scene in the 3rd person ("**This is** my beloved Son") — since one does not refer to oneself as "this" (a 3rd-person term referring to someone else), the speaker and the object are distinguished no matter which person is used to read it. Nor does this explain why the sound came from outside Jesus (heaven). If one answers, "God is omnipresent and so can be in multiple places at once," then in that instant the scheme "the soul is in heaven, the body is on earth" fails to divide anything — being fully present everywhere without division leaves the explanation that distinguished the three terms by location with nothing left to do. Conversely, if one answers, "only in that moment was He divided into three locations," that amounts to acknowledging that, for that moment at least, three independent beings existed, and the "one person" explanation does not hold.

**Verdict**: ❌ UNBIBLICAL — IRONCLAD (Luke 3:21-22, Mark 1:11, Matt 3:17)

## 6. The Claim That the Holy Spirit Is "Breath"

**The Doctrine's Claim**: the Holy Spirit is not a person but the Father's breath.

**Biblical Rebuttal**: in Acts 13:2, the Holy Spirit speaks in the 1st person — "separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto **I** have called them" — breath does not call itself "I" and ask that people be set apart unto itself. Acts 15:28 places the Holy Spirit alongside people, joined by a conjunction, in the council's decision letter: "it seemed good **to the Holy Ghost, and to us**." Rev 22:17 ("the Spirit and the bride say, Come") is an independent witness in which the same structure repeats with a different author. 1 Cor 12:11 uses a verb of will, saying the Spirit distributes "as **he** will," and Eph 4:30 says the Holy Spirit can be grieved. Matt 12:32 (blasphemy against the Holy Spirit being weightier than blasphemy against the Son) also cannot be explained by the "breath" account — this scheme has no answer for why offending against breath (respiration) would become a graver sin than offending against the Son. John 16:13 describes the Holy Spirit as the subject who "speaks what he hears" — hearing and relaying are not properties of breath (respiration) but of a personal agent. If one uses John 20:22 ("Jesus breathed on them, and saith, Receive ye the Holy Ghost") to establish "breath=Holy Spirit," a new problem arises within the scheme itself — following the structure of Gen 2:7 (§3), "the breath of life (spirit)" is a material combined into the body to make the soul, not a product emitted from the body, yet John 20:22 depicts the body (Jesus) breathing out the spirit (the Holy Spirit) — the direction is reversed. Moreover, as confirmed in §3, the very sentence structure of Gen 2:7 reverses this scheme, so the claim "the Holy Spirit = the Father's breath" is already unsteady from its own grounding verse.

**Verdict**: ❌ UNBIBLICAL — STRONG (Acts 13:2, Acts 15:28, Rev 22:17, 1 Cor 12:11, Eph 4:30, Matt 12:32, John 16:13, John 20:22)

## 6B. The Conflict Between the Holy Spirit's "Coming" and Omnipresence

**The Doctrine's Claim**: the Holy Spirit is the power/spirit of God who is already everywhere (omnipresent).

**Biblical Rebuttal**: John 16:7 — "it is expedient for you that I go away: for if I go not away, the Comforter will not come unto you; but if I depart, I will send him unto you." If the Holy Spirit were merely an already-omnipresent power, He would already be beside the disciples regardless of whether Jesus physically departed or not. Yet the text specifies Jesus's departure as a **necessary condition** for the Comforter's coming — an omnipresent energy cannot "come or not come." This shows that the Comforter is a distinct person who comes, at a specific point in time (Pentecost), bearing a specific mission.

**Verdict**: ❌ UNBIBLICAL — STRONG (John 16:7)

## 7. The Claim That the Holy Spirit Is Only "Power"

**The Doctrine's Claim**: Scripture most frequently refers to the Holy Spirit not as a person but as the power of God (2 Tim 1:7, Luke 1:35, etc.).

**Biblical Rebuttal**: it is true that the cited verses connect the Holy Spirit and power, but that connection does not require the conclusion "He is not a person." On the contrary, the Holy Spirit repeatedly appears as the subject of personal actions as well — being grieved (Isa 63:10), distributing "as he will" (1 Cor 12:11), and speaking in the 1st person (Acts 13:2). Between the observation "He is associated with power" and the conclusion "He is not a person" lies a gap the text does not fill.

**Verdict**: ⚠️ UNSUPPORTED (the observation is true; the superlative and ontological conclusion is unsettled)

> **A Related Argument — the Standard "Can Be Drunk = Impersonal"**: the argument that the expression of the Holy Spirit being "poured out and drunk" (John 7:37-39, etc.) shows an impersonal characteristic follows the same structure. Applying this standard consistently would also make Jesus — who told believers to "eat and drink" His own flesh and blood (John 6:53, 55, "my flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed") — an impersonal substance rather than a person. The predicate "can be drunk" is not a standard that distinguishes personhood. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL (John 6:53, 55)

## 8. Problems of Consistency in Grammatical Interpretation Rules

> Below are cases where the interpretive rules of the Godhead doctrine camp fail to hold when applied consistently across the whole text.

**8-1. Neuter Nouns and Personal Pronouns** — Claim: since the Greek word referring to the Holy Spirit (pneuma) is grammatically neuter, it must be received not with a personal pronoun but with "it" (the grounds for the Rom 8:16-type argument). **Rebuttal**: yet the Godhead doctrine side itself claims, elsewhere (Acts 20:28), that the relative pronoun "he" receives the same neuter noun (Holy Ghost) — that is, it breaks its own rule that "since it is neuter, it must be 'it.'" (As §8-2 shows, the actual grammatical antecedent of that "he" is not the Holy Spirit but God — but the Godhead doctrine side's claim itself is already self-contradictory regardless.) If the rule that neuter grammar excludes personal pronouns is true, then the Godhead doctrine side's own reading of Acts 20:28 fails to hold in the first place. → ❌ IRONCLAD

**8-2. The Subject of "He"** — Claim: the "he" of Acts 20:28 refers to the Holy Spirit (on the grounds that it is the nearest noun within the same verse) — meaning the Holy Spirit purchased the church "with his own blood." **Rebuttal**: **the answer rests solely on the literal wording of the standard text (KJV 1769).**

> Acts 20:28 — *"to feed the church of **God**, which **he** hath purchased with his own blood."*

① **The Antecedent**: the noun to which the relative clause `which he hath purchased` attaches is **the immediately preceding `God`.** `the Holy Ghost` is not in the same clause but much earlier (`over the which the Holy Ghost hath made you overseers`), with the closer noun `God` intervening between them. That is, **even applying the opposing side's own rule** that "the nearest noun is the antecedent," the answer is not the Holy Spirit but God.

② **Testing the Conclusion**: the conclusion "the Holy Spirit purchased with His own blood" directly conflicts with Luke 24:39 ("a spirit hath not flesh and bones") — Jesus Himself specified that "a spirit has no physical elements like flesh and bones," so if the Spirit (the Holy Spirit) had blood, He would no longer be a spirit but a body. → ❌ STRONG (Acts 20:28, Luke 24:39)

> 🚫 **This item does not use an original-language argument (revised 2026-08-24).** The first edition made its main argument *"the Greek original has no separate pronoun corresponding to the KJV's 'he.'"* But **the KJV 1769's `he` is not italicized** — it is not a translator's supplement but the text itself. `CREED_Override.md` **C-1** establishes the KJV as the final text, and permits the original languages only as a supplementary aid when internal comparison within the KJV does not resolve the matter. An argument that uses the original language to weaken the wording of the KJV text conflicts with this principle, and invites, in the field, the counter-attack: **"you say the KJV is the final authority, and then you erase the KJV with Greek?"** Since ① above is sufficient on its own, the original-language argument is discarded.

**8-3. The "Indwelling" Construction** — Claim: "the Father dwells in me" (John 14:10) is evidence of personal fusion. **Rebuttal**: the same "indwelling" construction is used in 2 Cor 13:5 and Rom 8:9-10 to describe Christ dwelling within the believer, and in that case it is not read as the believer and Christ fusing into a single person. The same construction must be read the same way. → ❌ STRONG

**8-3B. "Another Comforter"** — Claim: John 14:16-18's "I will come" is evidence that Jesus Himself also speaks as the Holy Spirit. **Rebuttal**: the very vocabulary of verse 16, "**another** (ἄλλος, a distinct object of the same kind) Comforter," already distinguishes Jesus from the Comforter — one does not call oneself, while speaking, "another." Decisively, verse 18's "I will come to you" is immediately specified in context by the very next verse (19), "Yet a little while... because I live, ye shall live also" — this is a description referring not to identification with the Holy Spirit, but to Jesus's own post-resurrection appearing. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL (John 14:16, 18-19, immediate context)

**8-4. "Seeing His Form"** — Claim: John 14:9's "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father" (to Philip) is evidence of the literal identity of Jesus=the Father. **Rebuttal**: in John 5:37 (spoken to unbelievers), Jesus says, "ye have neither heard his voice at any time, nor seen his **shape**" — even though these people were seeing Jesus's physical body with their own eyes. This means "the Father's shape" is not simply identical with seeing Jesus's physical body (if it were identical, seeing Jesus would automatically constitute seeing the Father's shape as well). Therefore the "seeing" of 14:9 must be read not as physically seeing a shape but in the revelatory sense of coming to know the Father through faith, and "having seen the Father" and "having seen the Father's shape" must be distinguished. This is also linked with John 6:46, "not that any man hath seen the Father, save he which is of God, he hath seen the Father" — the very structure in which Jesus Himself says "I have seen the Father" presupposes a personal distinction between the one who sees (Jesus) and the object seen (the Father) (one cannot say of oneself, "I have seen myself"). → ❌ STRONG (John 5:37, John 6:46, paralleled with John 1:18, "no man hath seen [God] at any time; the only begotten Son... hath declared him")

**8-5. The Future-Passive Naming Construction** — Claim: Matt 1:23's "they shall call his name Emmanuel" refers not to Jesus's name but merely to God. **Rebuttal**: the same grammatical structure ("shall be called" / "shall call his name") is granted as "His name" in Isa 9:6, and since Matt 1:21 has already named this same person "Jesus," the antecedent of Matt 1:23's "his name" is that same person as well. → not graded (for reference)

## 9. Problems of Coherence in the Body/Soul/Spirit Scheme

> These are points where the scheme itself — body=Jesus / soul=Father / spirit=Holy Spirit — fails to hold when substituted into the text.

**9-1. Col 2:9 and "the Heavenly Soul"** — Claim: the Father is God's soul, dwelling in heaven (in the scheme, Father=soul). **Rebuttal**: applying Col 2:9's "**all** the fulness of the Godhead dwells in [Jesus]" directly means the entire Godhead (including the soul) must be within Jesus. Placing the soul (the Father) separately in heaven does not match what this verse says by "all." → ❌ STRONG (Col 2:9)

**9-2. The Conditions of the "One Even While Separated" Analogy** — Claim: while a man's soul and spirit must be attached within the body, God is one even while separated, and simultaneously the analogy "just as man is one man, so God is one God" is also used. **Rebuttal**: Scripture calls the separation of spirit and body in a man **death** (James 2:26). If separation is death on the man's side, then an explanation in which separation is the normal state on God's side cannot be the same kind of analogy as man in the first place — the very premise of the analogy "as man is, so is God" collapses. → ❌ IRONCLAD (James 2:26)

**9-3B. 🆕 "The Spirit of Verse 8 Is a Man's Spirit"** — Claim: since 1 John 5:7 speaks of heaven (deity) and 5:8 speaks of earth (humanity), the "Spirit" bearing witness in verse 8 is not the Holy Spirit but **a man's spirit** set apart for the Incarnation. As grounds, it cites the "spirit of holiness" (lowercase) of Rom 1:4.

**Rebuttal**: this claim holds **only if one begins reading from verse 7.** Two verses earlier, the answer has already been written down.

> **1 John 5:6** — *"it is the Spirit that beareth witness, because the Spirit is truth."*

verse 8 counts "three that bear witness," and who the first of these is has **already been stated by the author in verse 6.** And that Spirit is called **"truth"** — a predicate that does not attach to a man's spirit. The same word "beareth witness" links verses 6 and 8, and only verse 7 lies between them.

**A Supplementary Signal**: measured against the standard text (KJV 1769) — 1 John 5:8 is a **capital `Spirit`**, and the grounding verse Rom 1:4 is a **lowercase `spirit`.** The premise itself — *"it makes no difference whether it's capital or lowercase"* — does not match the text. However, per `LEXICON_Bible.md` Section 7, **capitalization is used only as a supplementary signal, not the sole grounds** — the main grounds rest on the wording of verse 6.

→ ❌ UNBIBLICAL — **STRONG** (1 John 5:6)

**9-3. The Assignment of "Water and Blood" in 1 John 5:6-8** — Claim: the "water" and "blood" of verse 8 are divided respectively into properties of the body (Jesus) and the soul (the Father). **Rebuttal**: verse 6 has already attributed both the water and the blood to the single subject of Jesus Christ, saying "this is he that came by water and blood." Following the order of the text, there is no basis to divide them again in verse 8. → ❌ STRONG (1 John 5:6)

**⑷ Anticipated Counter-Rebuttal: "The Old Testament Father Is Merely a Covenantal/Figurative Title"** 🆕

> 🚨 This counter-attack is **the most natural response** to ⑴. It is prepared for in advance.

it is **granted** that "Father" in the Old Testament genuinely is a **covenantal title** expressing God as the One who cares for and redeems Israel. Jer 31:9's *"I am a father to Israel"* is clearly the language of a covenant relationship. **This point must not be denied.**

But the instant it is granted, **the burden shifts to the opponent.**

```
If the claim is that the two meanings are entirely different
   → the opponent must specify by which text or standard the two are divided
   → without such a standard, this becomes "dividing them only when convenient"

If the claim is that the two meanings are continuous
   → this runs into Isa 63:16's "thy name is from everlasting"
   → which conflicts with the explanation that fatherhood first arose at the Incarnation
```

**The issue is not whether "Father" carries covenantal meaning.** It is whether the fatherhood **before** the Incarnation and the fatherhood **after** it are **the same, continuous, or entirely different** — whichever answer is chosen, **the standard must be shown from the text.**

→ ⚠️ this item is not a rebuttal but a **burden shift.** If the opponent does not answer, ⑴ stands as-is.

**⑸ Anticipated Counter-Rebuttal: "'He Sent' Is a Proleptic Expression for the Foreordained Humanity"** 🆕

> 🚨 This counter-attack is a response to ⑵.

it is **granted** that Scripture has expressions speaking of something God will accomplish in the future **as though it had already happened.** Rom 8:29-30 recording something not yet fulfilled in the past tense is an example. **This idiom itself genuinely exists.**

But Gal 4:4 and 1 John 4:14 are **not sentences foretelling the future.**

> **1 John 4:14** — "**the Father sent the Son** to be the Saviour of the world"

this is **a sentence describing the relationship** between **the One sending** and **the One sent** — not a statement about it. That proleptic idiom advances the tense; whether that is happening here is a separate question from **what this sentence's object ("the Son") refers to.**

```
"He prepared the body to be called the Son"  → the language of a plan
"He sent the Son"                              → the language of a relationship
```

**The text is written in the latter form.** "Foreordination" is a possible answer, but since it is **an interpretation converting relational language into planning language**, **the grounds for that conversion must be shown from the text.**

→ ⚠️ this item is also a **burden shift.** If grounds are not shown, ⑵ stands as-is.

## 11. A Collection of Rebuttals to Individual Verses

**11-1. John 10:30, "I and my Father are one"** — Claim: evidence of ontological identity. **Rebuttal**: the Greek "one" (ἕν) is neuter (not the masculine εἷς meaning a single entity), and the same Jesus, in the same Gospel of John 17:11, 21-22, prays for the union of believers using the exact same word ("that they may be one"). Since there is no dispute that the believers' "oneness" is not a fusion of personhood, reading John 10:30 — by the same author, using the same word — as the same kind of union (a unity of nature and will) is consistent with the internal comparison of the text. The standard text defining "oneness" in marriage (Gen 2:24, "they shall be one flesh") follows the same pattern — this is a union that holds only on the premise that **two distinct parties** exist from the start, and Paul, in Eph 5:31-32, quotes this very verse directly for the relationship between Christ and the church, without speaking of a personal fusion. That "one" can refer to the union of two distinct parties is already acknowledged by the marriage analogy the Godhead doctrine side itself commonly uses. → ❌ STRONG

**11-2. John 5:18, "equal"** — Claim: evidence that they are completely and identically one. **Rebuttal**: the word "equal" itself logically requires at least two distinct terms being compared — the expression "X is equal to X itself" does not hold. (Note: the deity of Jesus itself is strongly affirmed in verses 19-23, independent of this rebuttal.) → the opposing claim is dismissed

**11-3. John 1:1, "with God" / "was God"** — Claim: one must choose between the relational ("with") and the essential ("was"). **Rebuttal**: "was with" (πρός) is a preposition indicating a relationship between two distinct referents, and nowhere in the KJV does the expression "was with himself" appear. "Was God" describes a separate layer (essence) — the text states both from the outset, and does not demand a choice between them. → a false dichotomy

**11-4. The Parallel Construction of 1 Cor 8:6** — Claim: pairing the Father and Christ in the same parallel structure means they are identical. **Rebuttal**: the verse (KJV) as a whole contains not 2 but **4 prepositional phrases** — for the Father: *"of whom [are] all things, and we **in** him"* ("of" = source for all things, "in" = belonging for us — **two different relational words**); for Christ: *"by whom [are] all things, and we **by** him"* (both all things and us alike use "by" = agency, **repeated without changing a single particle**). A parallel construction is a rhetorical device placing two terms side by side, not a declaration of identity. Even more decisive is that the text's structure is not symmetric but **asymmetric** — Christ's side unifies "by" across both all things and us, fixing it to a single role (agent), while the Father's side uses different relational words, "of" for all things and "in" for us. If this really were the same referent called by different labels, there is no explanation for why the author would unify the expression on Christ's ("the same referent's") side while splitting it only on the Father's side — the opposite of "different names for the same being," the text systematically distributes different grammatical roles to the two names. → ❌ STRONG

**11-5. Heb 1:3, "the express image of his person"** — Claim: since it is the image of His "person" (ὑπόστασις), they are the same being. **Rebuttal**: "image" is a possessive construction that presupposes distinction from its object. ὑπόστασις is a polysemous word carrying various meanings depending on context — "substance/essence," "confidence," "fundamental reality" — so it is difficult to declare it fixed to a single usage within the KJV. However, in this context of Heb 1:3, since "image" is the object a possessive attaches to, one can cautiously say only that "this is not an expression directly designating an individual being." → the opposing claim is dismissed (a weak rebuttal)

**11-6. The Citation of Heb 1:8-9 / Ps 110:1** — the expression "thy God" (Heb 1:9) is a triple-distinction structure — speaker, recipient, and "the recipient's God" — with no reflexive marker. Jesus Himself directly uses Ps 110:1 as evidence of the distinction between speaker and hearer (Matt 22:41-45). → ❌ STRONG

**11-7. Acts 20:28's "He"** — identical to §8-2. In the KJV wording `the church of **God**, which he hath purchased`, the antecedent of the relative clause is the immediately preceding `God`, and `the Holy Ghost` sits in a much earlier clause. **The original-language argument is not used, since it conflicts with C-1 (the KJV's final authority)** — see the end of §8-2 for the detailed reason. → ❌ STRONG

**11-8. The "Two Thrones" Problem** — Claim: since heaven has only one throne (Rev 4:2, 22:1), the Father and the Son cannot be separate persons. **Rebuttal**: in Acts 7:55-56, Stephen reports witnessing "the glory of God, **and** Jesus standing on the right hand of God," joined by "and," as two visually distinct objects ("saw/I see" — eyewitness testimony, not a conceptual statement). Rev 3:21 has Christ directly say, "even as I also overcame, and am set down **with my Father** in his throne" — an explicit statement that one throne is shared, "together," by the Father and the Son — the word "together" itself presupposes at least two persons. Rev 5:6-7 likewise describes the spatial interaction of the Lamb taking a book out of the hand of the One seated on the throne. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL — IRONCLAD (Acts 7:55-56, Rev 3:21, Rev 5:6-7)

**11-9. Matt 28:19's "Name" (Singular)** — Claim: "the **name** of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost" (singular) refers not to three persons but to three expressions of one God. **Rebuttal**: the Greek construction is "τοῦ πατρὸς **καὶ** τοῦ υἱοῦ **καὶ** τοῦ ἁγίου πνεύματος" — a structure joining three distinct genitive nouns in a row with a coordinating conjunction. If it were three "expressions" of one person, one would ordinarily expect an appositional construction or a single noun. The reading "one name shared by three" (a shared divine authority jointly possessed by three distinct persons) fits more naturally with the coordinated-listing structure, and this parallels the pattern in 1 John 5:7 of "the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost" (coordinated listing) + "one" (singular, union). → ❌ UNBIBLICAL

**11-10. Gen 1:26's "Our Image" + Isa 44:24's "I Alone"** — Claim: the "us" of Gen 1:26 is merely the Hebrew "plural of majesty" (the idiom of a king referring to himself as "we"), and Isa 44:24, "I... alone spread abroad the heavens," supports this by showing the Creator is a single person. **Rebuttal**: the plural of majesty is a real grammatical phenomenon, but its usage typically appears in ordinary declarative sentences (e.g., Ezra 4:18, "the letter which ye sent unto us"). By contrast, the "us" of Gen 1:26 is combined with a **cohortative verb** ("let us make"), a construction in which the speaker invites those included with him to act together, and which ordinarily presupposes the existence of a listener (Gen 11:7's "let us go down" follows the same pattern) — a structure grammatically different from the plural-of-majesty usage. Meanwhile, Isa 44:24 (in the context of chapters 43-45) is a monotheistic affirmation **rejecting idolatry and other gods (foreign idols)**, not a verse addressing the number of persons within God — "there is no God else beside me" (Isa 44:8, 45:5) does not conflict with the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit being together. → ⚠️ UNSUPPORTED (Gen 1:26's "plural of majesty" explanation does not precisely match the cohortative grammar, and Isa 44:24 answers a different question)

**11-11. Isa 42:1, the One Giving and the One Receiving the Spirit** — Claim: in "I have put my spirit upon him" (Isa 42:1), the distinction between the giver and receiver of the spirit is explained by "the two natures (deity, humanity)," and does not mean a distinction of persons. **Rebuttal**: the structure of this verse is a bestowal construction — "**the giving subject → the receiving object**" — the standard Old Testament anointing formula (1 Sam 16:13, Isa 61:1 = Luke 4:18). But **a nature cannot be the grammatical subject that gives or receives something** — giving and receiving are done by persons. The explanation "the deity gives the spirit to the humanity" does not explain the text's grammatical persons. Moreover, this bestowal structure repeats even **after** the resurrection and ascension — John 15:26, "whom I will send unto you from the Father, even the Spirit of truth, which proceedeth from the Father"; Acts 2:33, "having received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost, he hath shed forth this" (the receiver, the giver, and the thing given all appear together in one sentence — since this occurs after the ascension, the explanation "because of the humanity" is difficult to apply). → ❌ UNBIBLICAL (Isa 42:1, John 15:26, Acts 2:33)

**11-12. Acts 7:55-56, Stephen's Vision — The Limits of Applying the "Right Hand=Authority" Idiom** — Claim: since "the right hand" is an idiom meaning authority rather than a physical location (see §12), the scene Stephen saw must be read the same way. **Rebuttal**: this idiom originates from Luke 22:69's "the right hand of power" — and the parallel verses Mark 14:62 and Matt 26:64 show that the word "power" itself is a **Jewish-style euphemistic title substituted for God, avoiding direct use of the divine name** — that is, this is not "the right hand means power (an abstract concept)" but a possessive construction meaning "the right hand of the One who is power (God)." It is a sentence answering "whose right hand," not one defining "what is the right hand." More fundamentally, the rebuttal "since it's an idiom, there aren't really two locations" collapses on its own — the idiom "someone's right hand" is, by definition, an expression that holds only on the premise of two parties: the One **granting** that authority and the one **receiving** it (just as "the king's right-hand man" only has meaning on the premise that the king and the man at his right hand are distinct). Reading it as an idiom does not erase this two-party structure — rather, the idiom itself stands upon that structure. Moreover, the verses using this idiom **all describe "sitting," while Acts 7:55-56 alone describes "standing."** This is not an impression but an actual measurement.

> **[Full Scan: Text Search]** query `right hand of (God|power|the Majesty|the throne)` → **14 instances**
> ⚠️ Rev 5:1, Rev 5:7 (*"the right hand of him that sat on the throne"*) refer to **the hand of the One seated on the throne, in which He holds the book**, and were **excluded** from this comparison set. They are not the construction of someone "being at the right hand."
>
> | Posture | Count | Verses |
> |:---:|:---:|:---|
> | **Standing** (`standing`) | **2** | **Acts 7:55, Acts 7:56** — both the Stephen scene |
> | Sitting/seated/present (`sit`/`sat`/`set`/`is on`) | **12** | Matt 26:64, Mark 14:62, Mark 16:19, Luke 22:69, Acts 2:33, Rom 8:34, Col 3:1, **Heb 1:3, 8:1, 10:12, 12:2**, 1 Pet 3:22 |
>
> the 12-instance comparison set spans **6 authors** (Matthew, Mark, Luke, Paul, the author of Hebrews, Peter), with **the author of Hebrews alone repeating it 4 times.** This is how solidly "sitting" is fixed as the paired term of this expression, and the fact that this fixed pattern breaks in only one scene, in all of Scripture, makes that scene's description exceptional.
>
> **[A Byproduct — the Distribution of Modifiers]** the same 14 instances are also counted for "whose" right hand it is. `God` **8** / `power` **2** (Matt 26:64, Mark 14:62) / `the power of God` 1 / `the Majesty, the throne` 3.
> → **the §Development statement, "the text keeps substituting 'power,'" does not match this actual measurement.** Scripture's basic idiom is `the right hand of God`, and only two places use `power` alone. This distribution itself is a rebuttal asset (incorporated into the script's Turn section).

since an abstract concept has no posture, this difference in posture itself does not fit the reading "the right hand = purely abstract authority." Acts 7:55-56 is also not a theological statement but an eyewitness report of sight — "saw (εἶδεν)," "I see (θεωρῶ)" — explicitly naming "the glory of God **and** Jesus" as two objects joined by "and." → ❌ UNBIBLICAL — the idiom itself is valid, but does not apply to the eyewitness-testimony genre, posture description, and possessive structure of Acts 7:55-56 (Luke 22:69, Mark 14:62, Matt 26:64, Acts 7:55-56)

**11-13. Heb 7:1-3 Melchizedek = Jesus** — Claim: "without father, without mother" (Heb 7:3) literally describes the property not of the body but of the soul (the Father). **Rebuttal**: the decisive grammatical marker is "**made like unto**" (ἀφωμοιωμένος, a perfect passive participle). The author of Hebrews does not say Melchizedek "is" the Son of God, but explicitly states he "was made like unto" Him — this is grammar indicating a typological relationship, not a statement of ontological identity. "Without father, without mother" is paralleled with 7:6's "whose descent is not counted from them" — an argument grounded in the literary silence of Genesis 14 not recording Melchizedek's genealogy, not an ontological statement that he literally had no parents. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL — STRONG (Heb 7:3, 6, the "made like unto" grammar)

**11-14. Heb 1:8, "Unto the Son He Saith, Thy Throne, O God"** — the Father directly addresses the Son as "O God" (Heb 1:8) — a verse explicitly confirming the deity of the Son, cross-verified with John 1:1, John 20:28 ("My Lord and my God"), and Titus 2:13. However, this verse only settles the deity of the Son, and is neutral on whether the possessor of that deity is identical to the distinct person called "the Father" (see §12). → ✅ BIBLICAL — STRONG (Heb 1:8, John 1:1, John 20:28, Titus 2:13)

**11-15. "There Is No Son in Heaven"** — Claim: the fact that 1 John 5:7 records "the Word" instead of "the Son" is because, before the Incarnation, there was no distinct person called "the Son" in heaven. **Rebuttal**: Prov 30:4, long before the Incarnation (Old Testament), already asks, "what is his **son's** name, if thou canst tell?" John 3:13 ("the Son of man which is in heaven") describes the "Son of man (Son)" as being in heaven even while Jesus was on earth, and Heb 1:2 ("by whom also he made the worlds") explicitly names "the Son" as the agent of the work of creation. Mic 5:2 says the "**goings forth**" of the One who would come out of Bethlehem have been "of old, from **everlasting**" — the subject of the text is not a plan but His goings forth. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL — STRONG (Prov 30:4, John 3:13, Heb 1:2, Mic 5:2)

**11-22. 🆕 "Why Doesn't Scripture Ever Write 'the Right Hand of the Father'?" — The Real Reason for the Silence (Newly Established 2026-08-24)**

> **The Character of This Item**: what follows is **not a rebuttal but a shield.** It offers an **alternative explanation** for the opponent's argument from silence (*"since it never says 'the Father,' the Father does not exist separately"*) — it is not a positive argument proving the Father's existence. Confusing its use invites the reply, *"isn't that a guess too?"*

**The Opponent's Claim**: in the expression "the right hand of ~," Scripture never once says "**the Father's** right hand," using only "power" or "God." If the Father and the Son were two distinct persons, there would be no reason for this.

**The Observation Is True** — **[Full Scan: Text Search]** query `right hand of the Father` → **0 instances.** This expression does not exist in the KJV. This fact itself is granted at the outset.

**But the Reason for the Silence Lies Elsewhere — Scripture Says So Directly.**

**⑴ The Vocabulary Substitution Is Right There in the Text — Exodus 33**

when Moses pleads, *"I beseech thee, **shew me thy glory**"* (33:18), God says, *"Thou canst not see **my face**"* (33:20), and substitutes **glory, goodness, and name** instead. **"The face cannot be seen → the glory can be seen" is Scripture's own substitution**, and the text itself explains why the vocabulary of eyewitness reporting stops at "glory."

**⑵ Stephen's Own Vocabulary Confirms This — Within the Same Speech**

> **Acts 7:2** (the opening of the speech) — *"**The God of glory** appeared unto our father Abraham"*
> **Acts 7:55** (the end of the speech) — *"saw **the glory of God**, and Jesus standing on the right hand of God"*

*"the God of glory"* ↔ *"the glory of God"* — **these pair within a single speech.** Stephen's choice of the word "glory" did not narrow the referent — it was **the precise vocabulary available to him.**

**⑶ "The Father" Is Not a Name Applied by Visual Confirmation**

John 1:18 directly states the source of that title — *"the only begotten Son… **he hath declared him**."* **Making the Father known is the Son's own office.** Since it is not a name a witness of the throne could apply by visually confirming it, its absence from an eyewitness report is **exactly what is expected.**

**⑷ Then Why Does "the Right Hand of God" Appear as Many as 8 Times? (Repelling the Anticipated Counter-Attack)**

`right hand of God` is **not a report of what was seen, but a description of location.** Stephen reports having **seen** only two things — ① the **glory** of God, ② **Jesus.** *"On the right hand of God"* is a construction marking Jesus's location relative to God, not a statement that God was visually confirmed.

> 🔑 **And this very point reinforces §11-12.** The opponent tries to fold this into *"Christ alone, within the glory,"* but that reading cannot answer **"who is the reference point of 'the right hand'?"** Since two things were seen (glory, Jesus) and there is yet another reference point, the referent **still does not fold.**

**[An Honest Point of Tension]** Gen 32:30 (*"I have seen God face to face"*), Exod 24:10, Judg 13:22, and Isa 6:1, 5 say "God was seen." This is resolved by **the Old-Testament-theophany=Christ argument §12 has already adopted** — Judg 13:21 explicitly identifies that object as *"an angel of the LORD,"* and what John 1:18 and John 6:46 deny is having seen **the Father.** Also, Exod 33:11's *"face to face"* stands alongside **the same chapter's 33:20** ("I cannot see my face"), showing that the KJV itself does not use this expression as literal sight.

**Verdict**: ✅ BIBLICAL — **STRONG** (shield use) · cross-witness **4 authors** (Moses / John / Paul / Luke, quoting Stephen) · Difficulty 🟡
**Restriction on Use**: use only to **neutralize** the opponent's argument from silence. Do not use it as proof of the Father's existence.

**11-19. 🆕 "What Was Made in Mary's Womb Is Only a Body"** — Claim: God was not born — He merely entered into the body prepared in the womb (grounds: Heb 10:5, "thou hast prepared me a body"). **Rebuttal**: simply look at what the two verses of the very context cited as grounds call that thing.

> **Luke 1:35** — *"that **holy thing** which shall be born of thee **shall be called the Son of God**."* → the text calls it **the Son of God.**
> **Gal 4:4** — *"God sent forth **his Son**, **made of a woman**, made under the law."* → the grammatical object modified by `made of a woman` is **his Son**, and **the word `body` does not appear in this verse.**

two authors (Luke and Paul) state the same point in different books. Even the grounding verse, Heb 10:5, does not fully support this claim — *"a body hast thou prepared **me**"*'s "**me**" is a dative that presupposes the subject receiving the body already existed prior to that body. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL — **STRONG** (Luke 1:35, Gal 4:4, Heb 10:5)

**11-20. 🆕 The Referent of 1 Cor 15:24's "He Who Shall Subdue the Kingdom"** — Claim: in the latter half of 1 Cor 15:24, "when he shall have put down all rule and all authority and power," He refers to **the Father** (and thus, He who occupies the position of the Son is revealed to be Himself the Father). **Rebuttal**: this collapses in two ways.

① **The Referent Shifts Within a Single Sentence**: verse 24 reads *"when **he** shall have delivered up the kingdom to God, even the Father; when **he** shall have put down all rule…"* The earlier `he` **hands the kingdom over to the Father**, and so cannot be the Father — one cannot hand something over to oneself. Switching only the later `he` to the Father violates `DEOVERLAP_Serial.md` **Principle 7 (the Pronoun-Consistency Lock)**, and this is a problem at the sentence level, prior to any theological position.

② **The Very Next Two Verses**: this claim ordinarily stops citing at verse 26, but verses 27-28 contain the author's own answer.

> **1 Cor 15:27-28** — *"…manifest that **he is excepted**, which did put all things under him. And when all things shall be subdued unto him, then shall **the Son** also himself be **subject unto him** that put all things under him."*

`excepted` is, in this paragraph, **a word the author himself uses to explicitly state that two objects are distinguished**, not an interpretation added by the rebutter. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL — **STRONG** (1 Cor 15:24, 27-28)

> ⚠️ **Reason for Not Raising the Grade to IRONCLAD**: this doctrine's Version B (§9-4) already holds the structure in which **the humanity (the Son) obeys the deity.** On that premise, there is room for "the obedience of the Son" in verse 28 to be absorbed into the opponent's own explanation. Therefore verses 27-28 are not a knockout blow but **pressure**, and the center of gravity for this card rests on **the pronoun shift of ①.** Read together with §13-2's caution ("do not read submission as absorption").

**11-21. 🆕 The Timing of Ps 2:7's "This Day Have I Begotten Thee"** — Claim: Ps 2:7 refers not to the origin of the Son, but to **the point when God put on a human body and was born.** **Rebuttal**: the earlier part, "this is not a statement of origin," is correct (→ recorded as granted in §12). But **not a single** New Testament author attaches that "this day" to the virgin birth.

> **Acts 13:33** — *"in that he hath **raised up Jesus again**; as it is also written in the second psalm, Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee."* → Paul attaches it to the **resurrection** (confirmed by the very next verse, 13:34, *"he raised him up from the dead"*).
> **Heb 5:5** — *"Christ glorified not himself to be made **an high priest**; but he that said unto him, Thou art my Son, to day have I begotten thee."* → the author of Hebrews attaches it to **the appointment as High Priest.**

this is not the rebutter's interpretation of what Ps 2:7 means, but **the result of counting the places where that verse is actually cited within Scripture.** → ❌ UNBIBLICAL — **STRONG** (Acts 13:33, Heb 5:5)

**11-16. Mark 13:32, "Neither the Son"** — Claim: the Son "not knowing" that day and hour actually means He genuinely knows but lacks the "authority" to disclose it (a reinterpretation preserving the premise that the Father and Son are identical and so cannot possess different knowledge). **Rebuttal**: the verb this verse uses is "knoweth," not any vocabulary of the "lacks authority" type. The structure explicitly stating "neither the Son [even the Son does not know]" and contrasting it with "but the Father [only]" clearly frames this as a matter of the scope of knowledge. The reinterpretation "he lacks only authority but actually knows" substitutes the text's actual verb (know/not know) with an entirely different category (authority/role) that is not in the text. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL (Mark 13:32)

**11-17. 1 John 2:23-25, "He Hath Promised Us" (Singular)** — Claim: after the two names of the Son and the Father appear, being received by the singular pronoun "he" in 2:25 ("1+1=1") is evidence of a single being. **Rebuttal**: listing two singular nouns and then receiving one of them with a singular pronoun is an entirely ordinary grammatical phenomenon in both Greek and Korean, and does not indicate "one being combining the Father and the Son," but the ordinary pronoun usage of referring to one of the preceding nouns. More decisively, the same author's (John's) 2 John 1:9 writes that whoever abides in the doctrine of Christ "hath **both** the Father and the Son" — the expression "both" is grammatically valid only when there are two distinct referents, and this parallel expression from the same author, in the same body of correspondence, directly conflicts with the "one being" interpretation. → ❌ UNBIBLICAL (1 John 2:25, 2 John 1:9, "both")

**11-18. The Juxtaposition of Divine Attributes and Human Limitations** — the KJV records, side by side, verses describing God's attributes ("he that keepeth Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep," Ps 121:4; "neither tempteth he any man," James 1:13) and verses describing Jesus ("was asleep," Mark 4:38; "tempted," Matt 4:1; "gave up the ghost," John 19:30). Both series are literal KJV wording from five independent authors (David, James, Mark, Matthew, John), and preserving both without erasing either requires that two different layers of description hold simultaneously within Jesus. → ✅ BIBLICAL — STRONG (5 independent authors converging, confirming that "both series genuinely exist simultaneously")

> **Grounds for Exclusion**: Hab 1:12's "we shall not die" is a 1st-person petition (prayer) that Habakkuk offers to God, not a verse directly describing God in the 3rd person like Ps 121:4 or James 1:13 — it belongs to a different category and is excluded from this list.

## 13. Errors to Watch For When Composing Rebuttal Material

> Even among materials rebutting the Godhead doctrine, there are cases where inconsistent rule application has crept in. Using them as-is repeats the same kind of error pointed out in §8.

**13-1. The Selective Acceptance of Future-Passive Constructions**: if Isa 9:6 ("shall be called") is accepted as a name while Matt 1:23 ("they shall call his name"), despite being grammatically identical in structure, is excluded as not a name, this becomes the same double standard pointed out in §8-5. → **Correction**: grant that Matt 1:23 is also His name. The core argument (that deity is not born, and that being called a name and the beginning of existence are different questions — a tense argument) remains valid regardless of this concession.

**13-2. Reading 1 Cor 15:28's "Subject" as Absorption**: "be subject unto" is a relational verb that presupposes distinction, so reading it, in this verse alone, as absorption/merging becomes the same kind of misreading pointed out in §9. → **Correction**: stay with what the text actually says (subjection).

**13-3. The Misreading of Gal 4:14's Simile Construction**: "as an angel of God, even as Christ Jesus" is a simile describing how the Galatians received Paul, not a statement of ontological identity between angels and Christ. This verse has been cited incorrectly by both camps. → **Correction**: substitute Judg 13:21-22 (§12).

**13-4. 🆕 Original-Language Arguments That Erase KJV Wording**: an argument of the type "Greek has no such pronoun," which weakens KJV wording, conflicts with `CREED_Override.md` **C-1** (the KJV's final authority, the original languages as supplementary). This holds especially strongly **when the word in question is not italicized** (such as Acts 20:28's `he`), since it is then not even a translator's supplement. In the field, this invites the counter-attack, *"you say the KJV is the final authority, and then you erase the KJV with Greek?"* → **Correction**: resolve the matter first through KJV-internal comparison, and use the original languages as a supplement only when that fails to resolve it (see the revised §8-2).

**13-5. 🆕 Calling a Point Where the Opponent Is Correct "Wrong"**: the observation on Luke 1:31's "in the womb," the juxtaposition of the nine examples, and the 0-instance count of "Mother of God" — **when checked by full scan and literal comparison, the opponent is correct on all three** (§12). Striking here invites the single-line reply, *"you never even counted it,"* and **that one error collapses the credibility of every other rebuttal along with it.** → **Correction**: grant it at the outset. Granting it is not a loss — it is the price that purchases credibility for the remaining cards.

**13-6. 🆕 Throwing a Card Without Confirming the Scheme**: §1 (the soul at Gethsemane), §3 (the Gen 2:7 reversal), §9-1, and §9-2 are **exclusive to Version A.** Thrown at a Version-B opponent, they die with the reply, *"I never said that."* → **Correction**: target first with the two questions of §9-4-2, then choose a card.

**13-7. 🔴 Reading `Ruin (destroy/perish)` as `Annihilation (the Erasure of Existence)`** — **an error this reference collection actually made (occurred and withdrawn 2026-08-24).**

the draft cited two verses to rebut the claim *"the soul is not annihilated"*: Ezek 18:4, *"the soul that sinneth, **it shall die**"* + Matt 10:28, *"able to **destroy** both soul and body in hell."* **Neither holds.**

| Why It Collapses | Grounds |
|:---|:---|
| ⓐ `destroy` does not mean annihilation | that same word in Matt 10:28 is also **rendered `lost` elsewhere in the KJV** — Luke 15:24, *"he was **lost**, and is found"* (having been lost, he was **found**), Luke 15:4, 6, Matt 18:11, Luke 19:10, John 6:12, *"nothing be **lost**"*, John 17:12. What can be found has not been annihilated. John 3:16 and 2 Pet 3:9 render the same word `perish` |
| ⓑ `die` does not conflict with the opponent's own definition | the opponent has **already defined** death as **"separation."** Substituting that definition, Ezek 18:4 does not conflict with the opponent's own statement. **Trying to strike the opponent with a word the opponent had defined themselves** is the structural flaw of this card |
| ⓒ the text in fact favors the opponent | Luke 16:23-25 (consciousness continues) · Rev 20:10, 14:11 (*"for ever and ever"* torment) · Mark 9:44, 46, 48 (*"their worm **dieth not**"*, 3×) · Isa 66:24 |

→ **Correction**: this card is discarded, and moved to **granted, ✅** in §12. To address Version B's "the Father does not die, because He is the soul," one must ask not about the soul's immortality but **the equation "the Father = the soul"** itself — because there are **0** verses supporting that equation literally, and the sole sentence defining God's existence as "He is" is John 4:24, *"God [is] a **Spirit**."*

> 🚨 **General Rule**: when constructing a rebuttal card, do not use **a word the opponent has already defined** (here, "death = separation") as grounds. That word is the opponent's home ground. And **count the KJV's internal usage before assuming the meaning of a word** — that `destroy` is also translated `lost` would have surfaced with a single search.

## Opening — A Question to Begin

Friends, let's begin with a single question. **Is God one Person, or three?**

This is exactly the teaching called "the Godhead doctrine."

## Development — The Grounds for the Godhead Doctrine

First, let's look at the expression "sat down at the right hand of God." Matthew chapter 26, verse 64 — Jesus's own words, spoken directly before the high priest.

> "Jesus saith unto him, Thou hast said: nevertheless I say unto you, Hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the **right hand of power**, and coming in the clouds of heaven."

There's something worth noticing here. Jesus does not say plainly, "the right hand **of the Father**." He says "the right hand **of power**." Mark 14:62 records the exact same "right hand of power" in the exact same spot. Luke 22:69 writes "the right hand of **the power of** God."

Think about it. This is the most decisive moment of His entire life — the moment He declares before the high priest who He is. If the Father and the Son really were seated side by side, two distinct Persons, why, of all places, right there, would He say "the right hand of power" instead of "the right hand of the Father"? If anything, this sounds as though what Jesus received wasn't "a seat beside someone," but God's own authority itself. Doesn't it fit together far more naturally if the Father and the Son were never two separate Persons to begin with, but one — and so He received that authority in full, as His own? What do you think?

Let me give one more example. Suppose there had been a record, in Old Testament times, saying "the right wing supported King David." Of course, there was no such political party back then, but bear with me. "The right wing" here obviously doesn't mean spring onions, that green vegetable. "Right" clearly means "the right side," but here it doesn't refer to a direction or a vegetable — it refers to a certain leaning, a certain camp. "The right hand of power" is the same. It isn't really talking about a literal right-hand seat — it's a word pointing to authority and status.

*(An idiom is an expression that, rather than carrying its literal word-for-word meaning, has hardened over a long time into a different, specialized meaning. Just as "he has wide feet" doesn't mean his actual shoe size, but that he knows a lot of people.)*

**"To sit at the right hand of ~" is an idiom. Please remember this — it matters.**

Second, 1 Corinthians 2:11 is similar. "What man knoweth the things that belong to a man, save **the spirit of man which is in him**? **Even so** the things that belong to God knoweth no man, but only the Spirit of God." Look closely at the phrase "even so" here. Paul is placing man and God side by side and comparing them. Let me ask you this — is "my spirit" within me a separate person, distinct from me? No. It's simply myself. Yet Paul says God's Spirit is "even so." Just as a man's spirit is not another person separate from the man, God's Spirit is not another distinct Person separate from God either. That's a fairly persuasive argument, isn't it? What do you think?

**A man's spirit knows the things that belong to him, and God's Spirit likewise knows the things that belong to Him. Please remember this — it matters.**

Third, there is no record in Scripture of anyone praying to the Holy Spirit. There is no scene of directly addressing the Holy Spirit and **offering prayer or worship** — not anywhere, whether you search the New Testament or the Old. The program searched the entire Bible again, and not a single verse of prayer offered to Him turned up. Countless prayers are offered to the Father, and we're even told to pray in Jesus's name — but toward the Holy Spirit, there is not a single such record, not even once. If the Holy Spirit really were a Person, distinct from the Father and the Son in exactly the same way, how could there be no record anywhere in Scripture of anyone praying to Him? What do you think?

**The Holy Spirit has never received prayer. Please remember this — it matters.**

Fourth, let's turn to John chapter 14. The disciple Philip makes this request of Jesus — "Lord, shew us the Father, and it sufficeth us." And Jesus answers, "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father." Look closely. Philip asked to be shown "the Father," and Jesus answers, "look at me." If the Father and Jesus were truly two separate Persons, this answer itself would be a non sequitur. Answering the request "show us the Father" with "just look at me" — doesn't this itself mean that Jesus Himself is the Father? Isn't this fairly direct evidence? What do you think?

**Having seen Jesus is the same as having seen the Father. Please remember this — it matters.**

Of course, there are more grounds than these, but I've picked out the four hardest to rebut.

Isn't it clear — God is one Person, and the Father, the Holy Spirit, and Jesus appear as one and the same Person?

## Turn — But We Cannot Stop Here

But... we cannot stop here. We need to look deeper.

The grounds above alone are not enough to claim that God's spirit, soul, and body are structured exactly the same way as man's spirit, soul, and body.

Let me give an example.
This actually happened — my younger brother sometimes gets greeted, in Jongno, by people he doesn't know, and it puzzles him. Why do they greet him? He's big-built and has a serious head of curly hair, and he looks exactly like our father — like two peas in a pod. Those people thought he was our father and greeted him. But just because my brother was greeted in place of our father doesn't mean he actually becomes our father, does it? Resemblance and identity are two different things.

John 14:9 is the same. That seeing Jesus means having seen the Father, and that Jesus is Himself the Father in person, are two different claims.

In logic, this is called **the fallacy of equivocation**, or **lexical ambiguity** — using the same word in two different senses while passing it off as one. It's a slight swap between "seeing" and "being the same."

Here is the decisive blow. Look at John chapter 5, verse 37. Speaking to those who did not believe in Him, Jesus says this — "ye have neither heard his voice at any time, **nor seen his shape**." These people were, at that very moment, seeing Jesus's physical body with their own eyes. And yet Jesus says, "you have not seen his (the Father's) **shape**." What does this mean? It means that "the Father's shape" is not simply the same thing as seeing Jesus's physical body. If it were the same, then the moment they saw Jesus, they should automatically have seen the Father's shape as well — but Jesus does not say that.

So then what does 14:9 mean, spoken to the believing disciple Philip — "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father"? This isn't "having seen his shape" — it means the disciples saw the Father through Jesus. "Having seen the Father through Jesus" and "having seen the Father's shape with the eyes" are two different things — one is revelation (a disclosure), and one is physical sight.

The verse that shows this most decisively is John chapter 6, verse 46. "**Not that any man hath seen the Father, save he which is of God, he hath seen the Father.**" Look closely — Jesus is, at this very moment, saying of Himself, "**I** have **seen** the Father." "To see" is a verb that only holds when the one seeing and the object seen are distinguished. No one says of themselves, "I have seen myself." This single verse alone reveals that Jesus and the Father are distinguished as the one who sees and the one seen.

**Please remember this — it matters. Having seen Jesus's body (physical form) is not the same as having seen the Father's body (form).**

**And we need to look again at "the right hand of power" as well.**

Earlier in this video, I told you that Jesus said "the right hand **of power**," not "the right hand of the Father." That's true. But is that the way Scripture speaks throughout? **I counted. The expression "the right hand of ~" appears in fourteen places.**

| What It Calls It | How Many Places |
|:---|:---:|
| **"the right hand of God"** | **eight places** |
| "the right hand of power" | two places |
| "the right hand of the power of God" | one place |
| "the right hand of the Majesty," "the right hand of the throne" | three places |

**Only two places use "power" alone.** Matthew 26:64 and Mark 14:62 — and these two record **the same scene, in two different Gospels.** It's effectively a single occasion. By contrast, the places that pin it down as "**the right hand of God**" number **eight** — Mark 16:19, Acts 2:33 and 7:55, 56, Romans 8:34, Colossians 3:1, Hebrews 10:12, and 1 Peter 3:22.

So it can't be said that "Scripture keeps substituting the word 'power.'" **Scripture's basic idiom is "the right hand of God,"** and "power" is a phrase used in that one particular scene before the high priest. And Luke 22:69 goes further, combining the two into "the right hand **of the power of** God" — meaning that power itself points to God.

**Please remember this — it matters. "The right hand of power" appears in two places; "the right hand of God" appears in eight.**

**But here's the question we really need to ask — then why doesn't "the right hand of the Father" ever appear, not even once?**

That's right. I looked, and **there truly isn't a single place.** But is the reason "because the Father doesn't exist separately"? Scripture gives the answer directly.

**The Father is One whom no man can see.**

> Exodus 33:20 — "Thou canst not **see my face**: for **there shall no man see me, and live**."
> 1 Timothy 6:16 — "**Whom no man hath seen, nor can see.**"
> John 6:46 — "**Not that any man hath seen the Father**, save he which is of God."

I already read you John 6:46 earlier. **Jesus is the only One who has seen the Father.**

So think about it. Stephen is, at this moment, saying, **"I am seeing this right now."** This is an eyewitness report. But the Father is One who cannot be seen. **One cannot report "I saw" someone who cannot be seen.** So the furthest word Stephen could use was — "**the glory of God**."

That this isn't a forced reading, Scripture shows in two places.

**First, this exact vocabulary substitution is right there in Scripture.** In Exodus 33, Moses pleads — "**shew me thy glory**" (verse 18). God answers — "**my face cannot be seen**" (verse 20). And He substitutes **glory, goodness, and name** instead. **"The face cannot be seen, but the glory will be shown"** — this is Scripture's own idiom.

**Second, Stephen's own habit of speech confirms it.** At the very start of that same speech, Acts 7:2, what does Stephen call God — "**the God of glory**." And at the end of the speech, he says he saw "**the glory of God**." **It matches, front to back, within a single speech.** Stephen did not narrow the referent — he used **the precise word he had available.**

And Scripture also tells us where the name "the Father" comes from. It's John chapter 1, verse 18 — "no man hath seen God at any time; the **only begotten Son... hath declared him**." **"The Father" is a name that the Son makes known.** It is not a name someone who witnessed the throne applied by visually confirming it.

You might ask in return — "then why does 'the right hand **of God**' appear as many as eight times? Isn't God equally unable to be seen?"

But look closely. What Stephen reports having **"seen" is two things** — **the glory of God**, and **Jesus.** "On the right hand of God" is **a phrase marking where Jesus is located** — it is not a statement that God was seen with the eyes. It's an expression of position, not an eyewitness report.

**And this is exactly where it matters.** Read that way, one question remains — **if what was seen is two things, "glory" and "Jesus," and Jesus is at that "right hand," then who is the reference point for that right hand?** Folding it into "Christ alone, present within the glory" leaves this question unanswered.

**Please remember this — it matters. The absence of the phrase "the right hand of the Father" is not because the Father doesn't exist — it's because the Father is One whom no man can see.**

Now let's turn to the story of Stephen.
**Acts chapter 7 — Stephen is about to be martyred by stoning.**
Scripture records it this way — "he, being full of the Holy Ghost, looked up stedfastly into heaven, and saw the glory of God, and Jesus standing **on the right hand of God**." The very next verse confirms it again — "Behold, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing **on the right hand of God**."

There's something strange here. Every "right hand" verse we've seen so far — Matthew 26:64, Mark 16:19, Luke 22:69 — all say "**sat down**." But this scene with Stephen alone, and across two consecutive verses at that, says "**standing**."

Do you remember what I said an "idiom" was, earlier in this video? I said it's a **fixed expression** — not the literal meaning of the words, but a specialized meaning hardened over long use. "Wide feet" means having many connections, but if you said "big feet," that would just mean literal shoe size — because an idiom is a fixed expression, changing a word within it breaks its special meaning.

Here's exactly where we need to draw a careful distinction. The positional phrase "**the right hand**" genuinely is an idiom — a phrase pointing to a seat of authority and glory. But "**standing**" is different. This is not part of that idiom — it's a literal description of the actual action that took place.

**I counted every place this idiom is used. There are fourteen.** Of those, **twelve say "sat down"** — Matthew 26:64, Mark 14:62 and 16:19, Luke 22:69, Acts 2:33, Romans 8:34, Colossians 3:1, Hebrews 1:3, 8:1, 10:12, and 12:2, and 1 Peter 3:22. This was written by **six recorders**, and **the writer of Hebrews alone repeats it four times.** "Sit" is the word originally built into this idiomatic expression. But Stephen's scene alone, and across two consecutive verses at that, says "**standing**." "The right hand" still means a seat of authority, but "standing" is what the One in that seat actually did. **The seat is idiomatic, the action is literal** — this is a sentence combining both.

Stephen's scene also differs in genre. The other verses are theological declarations — "He sat down and received authority." But what Stephen says here is not that kind of declaration — it's a 1st-person eyewitness report: "I am seeing this right now." Indeed, the original text uses the visual verbs "saw / I see."

You may understand "the seat" as an idiom, if you like. But the actual action of "rising to stand" is something only a person, with a body and a will, can do. And this verse places "the glory of God" and "Jesus standing beside it" side by side with "and," reporting two distinct objects that were seen. Stephen is, at that moment, actually seeing, with his own eyes, the glory of God and Jesus standing beside it.

**Please remember this — it matters. "The right hand" is an idiom, but "standing" is a literal action — and the One who performed that action was witnessed as two distinct objects, side by side with "the glory of God."**

You might object — "if the right hand is an idiom too, then isn't 'standing' just part of that same idiom?" But this is the fallacy logic calls "hasty generalization" — extending the conclusion proven for one thing (the right hand) to an entirely different word (standing) without grounds.

You might also object this way — "you said the right hand is an idiom. So isn't this too just about authority, not two literal locations?" But even reading it as an idiom, the problem remains exactly the same. The expression "someone's right hand" itself presupposes two parties — the One **granting** that authority and the one **receiving** it. Even "the king's right-hand man" only has meaning on the premise that "the king" and "the person who is the right hand" are two distinct beings. Reading it as an idiom does not make this two-party structure disappear — rather, the idiom itself stands upon that very structure.

You might also answer this way — "standing could also be symbolic, couldn't it represent Him rising to welcome a martyr?" But even that answer offers no escape. "Rising to welcome someone" is itself already the action of a person with a body and a will. An abstract concept called "authority" cannot rise to welcome anyone. If anything, this answer amounts to conceding, on its own, that Jesus welcomed Stephen as a person. Read it as an idiom, and the posture goes unexplained; read it as symbolic action, and that very action presupposes a person — either way you answer, it's a dead end.

Picture this scene. Stephen looks up into heaven. There, **the glory of God is present**, and **Jesus stands at the right hand of that God.** Not one Person but two, each in a different location, seen at the same time. If the Father and the Son were one Person, this very picture could not be drawn — it would become a picture of someone standing at their own right hand.

You might answer this way — "it doesn't say 'God' here. It says 'the **glory** of God.' So what Stephen saw was Christ alone, present within the glory of God."

But even that answer offers no escape. Read the sentence **all the way through.**

> "the glory of God, **and**, standing **on the right hand of God**, Jesus"

Three things appear in one sentence. **The glory of God** is one; **Jesus** is one; and God, at whose **right hand** Jesus stands, is another one. You've seized on "the glory of God" and passed over "the right hand of God" that follows. And in the very next verse, "the right hand of God" appears **once more.** Two consecutive verses.

No one can stand at their own right hand.

An even more decisive statement is Revelation 3:21. Jesus Himself says directly — "even as I also overcame, and am set down **with my Father** in his throne." This one word, "together," already presupposes at least two. No one alone can use the word "together."

**Now let's turn to the Garden of Gethsemane.** Matthew chapter 26, verse 38.

> "**My soul** is exceeding sorrowful, unto death."

Some Bibles render this passage as "my mind is deeply troubled," but the King James Bible clearly says "**my soul**." Under the Godhead doctrine, the soul is the Father. That would mean the One grieving in this moment is the Father.

Look at the very next verse, 26:39.

> "Not as **I** will, but as **thou** wilt."

"I" and "thou" — a dialogue structure between two distinct persons, facing each other.

Here you might answer — **"that soul is not the Father — it's the human soul possessed by the humanity."** There are indeed those who explain it exactly that way.

**I'll accept that answer.** Then let me change the question. **How do you explain the will being split in two?**

"Not as I will, but as thou wilt." Here, the side **that does not want** and the side **that has resolved to obey** are distinguished. But having a will, wanting something, resolving to yield — these are things **a person does.** You said having a soul doesn't mean being a person. That's correct. **But having a will and being split is a different matter.**

If the humanity's side had **its own will** that it then set aside — then the humanity is something possessing a will. If the humanity's side had **no** will — then "not as I will" has nothing left to do.

**⟶ Which of the two is it?** If this were the body's soliloquy to its own soul, why wouldn't it be written in the 1st person — "my body wants to avoid this, but my soul obeys"? Looking at Romans chapter 7, a person's real internal conflict is written in the 1st-person singular — "I... myself...". But this prayer at Gethsemane is a 2nd-person dialogue, "I" facing "thou."

**Now let's turn to the courtroom.** 1 John 5:7 — "there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, and the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." This camp explains this away as "just one Person testifying to Himself."

But look at what Jesus Himself says directly in John 8:17-18. "It is also written in your law, that the testimony of **two men** is true. **I am** one that bear witness of myself, and **the Father that sent me** beareth witness of me." What did the Pharisees say right before this? "Thou bearest record of thyself; **thy record is not true**." This was an objection that self-testimony is legally invalid. If the Father and Jesus were one Person, Jesus could simply have said, "No, my own testimony is valid too." But Jesus doesn't do that — He deliberately cites the law requiring **two persons**, and sets up two witnesses: "I" and "the Father that sent me." If this were the self-testimony of a single Person, there would have been no reason to bring up this law at all.

**And right here, you might also answer this way** — *"the 'Spirit' appearing in verse 8 is not the Holy Spirit. It's a **man's spirit**, set apart for the Incarnation."*

But if you answer that way, **all you need to do is go back one verse.** That's what happens when you read starting from verse 7.

> **1 John 5:6** — "it is **the Spirit that beareth witness**, because **the Spirit is truth**."

Verse 8 counts "three that bear record." Who the first of these is, **John already wrote down two verses earlier.** And he calls that Spirit **"truth."** No one calls a man's spirit "truth."

**Please remember this — it matters. The answer isn't in verse 8 — it's in verse 6.**

**Let's turn to 1 John chapter 1, verse 3.**

> "truly our **fellowship** is **with the Father**, and **with his Son Jesus Christ**."

"Fellowship" — in Greek, *koinonia*. Friends, have you ever seen someone looking into a mirror, "having fellowship" with themselves? The very word "fellowship" only holds when there's someone other than yourself. But John lists the object of this fellowship as two — the Father and the Son. And exactly as you just heard — "**with the Father**," "**with his Son Jesus Christ**" — **"with" is repeated twice.** He did not bundle it into a single occurrence. In Greek as well, repeating a preposition this way is the standard grammar used to clearly distinguish two objects. **In the Korean King James Bible, that repetition is preserved exactly as it stands.**

**Now, to Genesis.** Chapter 2, verse 7.

> "the LORD God formed man of **the dust of the ground**, and breathed **the breath of life** into his nostrils; and man became a **living soul**."

Here the word **"soul"** appears, exactly as it stands. Some Bibles render this passage with the single word "a living creature," so "soul" doesn't appear — but the King James Bible clearly writes "**a living soul**." In this discussion, that's decisive.

Let's confirm one thing first, before going further. **Do you hold that God consists of three elements?** If you say yes, I'll continue. If you say no, we'll move past this part — but the questions I've already asked will still stand.

If you say three, let's substitute this directly into that scheme. Breathe spirit (breath) into a body (dust), and it becomes a soul — that would mean adding spirit (the Holy Spirit) to body (Jesus) produces soul (the Father). That's a completely backward conclusion — that the Father is the resulting product of combining Jesus and the Holy Spirit. It's like reading a recipe backward — you mix flour and eggs to make bread, but you can't then say, "so flour is bread."

Remember, earlier in this video — "just as a man's spirit knows what belongs to itself, so also God's Spirit knows, in the same way" — man and God were tied together with "in the same way." Paul was comparing man and God on exactly one single point — "the ability to know." But extending that into "so the way they're composed must be identical too," and claiming that just as man is made of spirit, soul, and body, so is God — that's where the problem arises. This very verse, Genesis 2:7, shows exactly how that "man" was actually made — "body (dust) + spirit (breath) = soul." Let's substitute this directly into God. If we apply it "in the same way," with body being Jesus and spirit being the Holy Spirit — **the terrible conclusion follows that God the Father was not a Being who existed from the beginning, but a "product" formed by the combination of Jesus and the Holy Spirit.** It was said to be "the same way" — but here, it is not the same way at all.

**Please remember this — it matters. God is a just and fair God. Shouldn't the way we interpret His Word be just and fair as well? Pushing the phrase "in the same way" as far as it will go when convenient, and quietly withdrawing it when it becomes inconvenient — that cannot be called fair interpretation.**

**Next is the scene at the baptism.** Luke 3:21-22. Jesus is in the water. The Holy Spirit descends **upon** Jesus in the bodily form of a dove. A voice is heard **from heaven** — "Thou art my beloved Son." Three points — earth, above Jesus, and heaven — appear simultaneously within one scene. The explanation "it was just the soul speaking to the body" addresses only two of those points (soul and body). It offers no explanation at all for why the Holy Spirit would need to descend "upon" Jesus in a bodily form distinct from Jesus.

You might answer this way — "God is great, and different from man. Man mustn't be separated, but God can be one even while separated."

But answering this way leads to a dead end. At the very start of this video, you began the entire discussion with exactly that analogy, "just like man" — "man was created in the image of God, so just as man is spirit, soul, and body, so is God." Now, when it's convenient, you say "God is different from man," and earlier, when it was convenient, you said "the same as man." Using the same analogy when convenient and dropping it when inconvenient — that is a double standard.

And what does Scripture call it, when spirit and body separate from man? James 2:26, "**the body without the spirit** is dead." For man, separation is death. But if you say separation is normal for God, that means man and God cannot be the same kind of analogy to begin with. The analogy itself — "as man is, so is God" — collapses.

Let me note one more thing. I mentioned earlier that there is no recorded prayer directly addressed to the Holy Spirit in Scripture. But going from "there is no recorded prayer to the Holy Spirit" all the way to "the Holy Spirit is not a person" is actually a huge leap. This is called the argument from silence. It's like a courtroom where silence isn't accepted as evidence. A single fact of absence does not, by itself, constitute proof of nonexistence.

But you also need to look at how Scripture describes the Holy Spirit. 1 Corinthians 12:11 — the Holy Spirit distributes gifts "as he will." Ephesians 4:30 — the Holy Spirit can be grieved. Having a will, being grieved — these are properties of a person, not properties of a power. A single silence, of not having received prayer, cannot erase all these many personal characteristics.

---

**But there's one thing that absolutely must be addressed here.**

Everything I've rebutted so far has been the scheme "the soul is the Father, the body is Jesus, the spirit is the Holy Spirit." But circulating alongside this teaching is **an explanation that assigns the labels differently.** Like this — "the Father is **the deity**, the Son is **the humanity**. As a result of God taking on human nature, the relationship called Father and Son came to exist."

Put this way, the Gethsemane rebuttal I just made doesn't apply. You could simply answer, "that soul isn't the Father — it's **the human soul**." The Genesis 2:7 rebuttal is the same — the labels being substituted have changed.

So this explanation requires **different questions.** Let me ask just three.

**First, since when has the name "Father" been used?**

If fatherhood **arose** when Jesus was born, then before that, God could not have been called Father. But counting through the Old Testament, there are places calling God Father, spanning **eight books, eleven times** — Deuteronomy, 2 Samuel, 1 Chronicles, the Psalms, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Malachi. And Isaiah 63:16 nails it down explicitly.

> "O LORD, thou art our father, our redeemer. **Thy name is from everlasting.**"

if the name "Father" arose later, "from everlasting" has no place to stand.

You might answer this way — **"that's the Father-relationship between God and Israel. What I meant is a relationship within God. It's a different level."**

But answering this way leads to a dead end. **Because it was you yourselves who connected those two levels into one.**

When you apply the "everlasting Father" of Isaiah 9:6 to Jesus, the very grounds you use is precisely **that Old Testament fatherhood** — *"in the Old Testament, the One who made Israel His firstborn son is God, and He made him a son, so that One would become the Father."* And you go on to say, *"later, the people of Israel will come to recognize, 'this is the One who made us His firstborn.'"*

**If you're going to claim that that very Old Testament Father is this One, the two levels must already be connected.** You cannot connect them when it helps you and separate them when rebutted.

**Second, if there's no Son, how can He send the Son?**

1 John 4:14 — "the Father sent **the Son** to be the Saviour of the world; and we have seen and testify." Galatians 4:4 likewise says, "God sent forth **his Son**."

To send someone, **the one sent must already exist.** If the name "Son" arose at birth, then at the moment of sending, there was not yet anyone to send.

You might answer, "it was sent within foreordination," but Galatians 4:4 pins down the timing explicitly — "**when the fulness of the time was come**." If it were talk of a plan, there would be no reason to pin down a specific timing.

**Third, would you read just two more verses?**

You read 1 Corinthians 15 through verse 26, and say "He who shall subdue the kingdom is the Father." Here are the very next two verses.

> "He that put all things under him is **manifest that he is excepted**. ... then shall **the Son also himself** be **subject unto him** that put all things under him."

"Excepted" is not an interpretation I've added — it's **the word Paul himself wrote.** To be excepted means to be distinguished from the rest.

And look again at verse 24 as well. It reads, "**when he** shall have delivered up the kingdom to God, even the Father." The One who **hands over** the kingdom to the Father cannot be the Father. One cannot hand something over to oneself.

---

**Here, let me say one thing fairly.**

You might say, "we've always taught it this way — we're just explaining it in more detail this time." **I'll grant that there's something to that.** The expression "in the humbled position, called the Son" is a phrase used before as well, and it's plausible that the idea of two natures grew from that seed.

But an explanation **growing more detailed** and an earlier statement being **reversed** are different things.

For example, there's this. This teaching has said, "**nowhere in Scripture is there dialogue exchanged between the Father and the Son.**" But elsewhere, regarding John chapter 12, it explains that "**God answered from heaven.**"

Turning what was said to be "absent" into "present" is not a more detailed explanation. **It's a withdrawal of what was said before.** "Nowhere at all" is either true or false — it isn't the kind of statement that gets softened by further detail.

**So I'll leave just this one question.**

> **If the scheme came from the biblical text, and the text hasn't changed, why has the scheme changed?**

Matthew 26:38, John 12:28, Genesis 2:7 — all unchanged. Not a single letter has changed. But if the explanation attached to those texts has changed — then what changed is not the text, but the **explanation.**

**Please remember this — it matters. If the text stays the same while the explanation changes, that explanation was not derived from the text — it was laid on top of it.**

---

**Now, let me show you solid evidence supporting the Trinity.**

Jesus mentioned Himself and the Holy Spirit side by side. Matthew 28:19 — "baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost." Jesus, with His own mouth, named all three together.

The Holy Spirit appears in Peter's epistolary greeting. 1 Peter 1:2 — "through sanctification of the Spirit... Grace unto you, and peace, be multiplied." The Father, the Spirit, and Jesus Christ, all three, appear together in one sentence.

The Father and the Son even converse with each other. John 12:28 — Jesus prays, "Father, glorify thy name," and from heaven comes the answer, "I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again." The dialogue between the two is recorded, plainly, as it stands.

The Holy Spirit also speaks directly. Revelation 22:17 — "the Spirit and the bride say, Come" — a passage where the Holy Spirit speaks side by side with the bride. In Acts 13:2 as well, the Holy Spirit speaks directly — "separate me **Barnabas and Saul** for the work whereunto **I** have called them, to be sent unto **me**." **"I,"** and **"unto me"** — the 1st person. Breath does not call itself "I," nor ask that people be set apart unto itself.

**Please remember this — it matters. The Holy Spirit spoke directly in Acts, and spoke alongside the bride in Revelation 22. He is the One who intercedes for us, and He is One who can be grieved.**

Friends, throughout Scripture, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit appear, again and again, distinct from one another — calling on one another, speaking to one another, each speaking in His own right. This is the ground on which we believe in the Trinity.

## Appendix 2 — Anticipated Q&A: "The Event of Jesus Being Tempted" + 5 Preparations for Deity/Humanity Rebuttals

> The content below takes one argument treated in a different sermon video, anticipates in advance how the Godhead doctrine side might rebut it, and attaches a counter-rebuttal to that anticipated response. This is not an actual conversation but a **hypothetical Q&A**, and its purpose is "not to settle the matter after hearing only one side." Where the Godhead doctrine side's rebuttal carries its own genuine logic, this is honestly acknowledged, and only then is what is still missing pointed out.

### Question: "Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God" — Who Is Jesus Speaking of Here?

Matthew chapter 4 — the scene of Satan tempting Jesus in the wilderness. Satan cites Psalm 91, urging, "if you are the Son of God, then test whether the Father will really protect you." Jesus answers.

> "Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God" (Matt 4:7)

**The Naturally Straightforward Meaning**: here, "the Lord thy God" refers to **the Father**, the subject of the promise in Psalm 91. That is, Jesus rebukes Satan, saying, "you [Satan] shall not tempt [the Father]." Since Jesus and that object (the Father) are distinct, this makes natural sense.

**But What Happens If We Substitute the Godhead Doctrine's Scheme (Jesus = God the Father Himself) Directly Here?** "the Lord thy God" = the Father = Jesus Himself. Then this sentence would come to mean the following.

> "You shall not tempt **me (Jesus)**."

The problem is that, at that very moment, Jesus is actually being tempted by Satan (Matt 4:1, "Jesus... to be tempted of the devil"). And James 1:13 states plainly, "God... neither tempteth he any man." Read according to the Godhead doctrine's scheme, Jesus would be simultaneously warning "do not tempt me" **while actually being tempted at that very instant** — a situation in which His own warning is undone by Himself.

---

### A Concept to Understand First: What Are "Deity" and "Humanity"?

The tool the Godhead doctrine side almost always brings out to block this kind of contradiction is the "deity/humanity distinction." Since this term will be unfamiliar to many, let's unpack it simply first.

```
deity (神性) = "the properties God originally possesses as God"
   e.g., never tempted, knows all things,
         is everywhere, never grows weary, cannot die

humanity (人性) = "the properties possessed as a man"
   e.g., grows hungry, grows weary, can be tempted,
         can only be in one place, can die
```

The word covering both together is **"nature"** — that is, "nature" is an umbrella term, and beneath it are two kinds, "deity" and "humanity."

```
nature (a general term meaning quality/attribute)
 ├─ deity = the God-side quality
 └─ humanity = the man-side quality
```

The original claim of this framework runs as follows — "Jesus is one Person, but within that one Person He simultaneously possesses these two qualities (deity and humanity)." As an analogy, it's similar to how one person can simultaneously be "a company's CEO" and "someone's child." In the boardroom, signing off on decisions, the "CEO" side shows; in front of their parents, the "child" side shows — the person is one, but depending on the situation, a different quality (role) surfaces.

The reason orthodox theology uses this framework is to explain, together, biblical verses that at first glance seem mismatched — "Jesus grew weary" and "Jesus knew all the thoughts of man's heart," for instance. The Godhead doctrine side **borrows this exact framework as well**, and uses it to block the contradictions its own scheme (Jesus = God the Father Himself) creates.

---

### Anticipated Rebuttal: "Not Received as Deity — Received as Humanity"

Since this is a line of reasoning the Godhead doctrine side has actually already used elsewhere (the Gethsemane-prayer problem) and is easy to reuse, **it is highly likely they would go straight to this same answer for this argument as well.**

> "Jesus possessed both deity and humanity together. As deity, He was not tempted; as humanity, He was tempted. James 1:13 speaks of deity, Matthew 4:1 speaks of humanity — so both are true, and there's no contradiction. Being tempted for 40 days was also something He actually underwent as humanity."

**This Rebuttal Is Fairly Sophisticated**: that "two layers of description coexist within one Person" is, in fact, a phenomenon Scripture itself shows (see §11-18 — we grant this ourselves). Since it borrows a tool from orthodox theology, its first impression is plausible.

---

### Counter-Rebuttal 1: "Then When Does the 'Soul (the Father)' Ever Appear in Jesus's Life?"

Continuing to use the "deity/humanity distinction" card reveals a problem of its own making.

```
grieving and being troubled at Gethsemane  → "that's the humanity (the body)"
being tempted                              → "that's the humanity (the body)"
dying on the cross                         → "that's the humanity (the body)"
praying                                    → "that's the humanity (the body)"
```

Under the Godhead doctrine's scheme, "the soul" is precisely "the Father." Yet looking at the list above, every actual event of Jesus's life is explained only on the "humanity (body)" side, and **the "soul (the Father)" side never once actually undergoes anything.** Every time, the escape is "that's exempt, because it's deity."

> **"If the body (humanity) and the soul (the Father) truly are 'one Person,' why do all the actual experiences a person undergoes pile up only on the body's side, while the soul's side has, across an entire lifetime, not a single recorded experience of anything?"**

---

### Counter-Rebuttal 2: "If the Humanity Had a Will, Grieved, and Resisted — Isn't That Itself the Very Definition of 'Personhood'?"

This is an even more fundamental hole. The Godhead doctrine side's own material has already laid out the standard defining personhood.

> "Personhood means being a being that has intellect, emotion, and will."

Let's hold this standard directly against what "the humanity" actually did.

```
Will   → Gethsemane: "not as I will, but as thou wilt"
          ("my will" ≠ "thy will" — two distinct wills appear)

Emotion → "my soul is exceeding sorrowful, unto death" (Matt 26:38)

Intellect → an immediate, logical response to Satan's third temptation (Matt 4:7)
```

Intellect, emotion, and will — all three appear. But since it was defined that "personhood means having intellect, emotion, and will," **applying that very standard means "the humanity" itself already fulfills every condition of personhood.**

The only escape route available to the Godhead doctrine side here is the distinction, "a nature is not a person — it is merely an attribute a person possesses." (Yes, this is that same "nature" mentioned earlier — the claim that whether deity or humanity, both are "attributes," not "the person itself.") This distinction itself does exist in orthodox theology too, so it is not entirely without grounds. The problem is that **while calling it "merely a nature" in words, in the actual explanation, the humanity is continually treated as "a subject that feels, decides, and resists."**

```
"the humanity was tempted"        → requires a 'subject' who undergoes temptation
"the humanity's will differed"    → requires a 'subject' who possesses a will
"the humanity grieved"            → requires a 'subject' who feels emotion
"the humanity answered Satan"     → requires a 'subject' who speaks
```

An attribute (a nature) cannot, on its own, feel, decide, or speak. Only a 'subject' (a person) can do that. So the moment one constructs sentences like "the humanity did this, felt that," one has already treated the humanity as a person. It is a self-contradiction — denying "not a person" in words, while continuing to describe it, in sentence structure, exactly as a person.

**Verdict**: ⚠️ the Godhead doctrine side's "deity/humanity distinction" rebuttal, borrowing orthodox theology's framework, is sophisticated at first glance. But it collapses in two ways — ① applying that framework repeatedly to actual events reveals the absence: "the soul (the Father)" never once actively appears across Jesus's entire life, and ② more fundamentally, the very manner of explaining "the humanity" already fulfills the conditions of a 'person' possessing intellect, emotion, and will, directly conflicting with the premise that it is "merely a nature, not a person."

### Counter-Rebuttal 3: "Then Was It the Deity and Humanity Conversing With Each Other?" — Whether Heaven or Earth

Once Counter-Rebuttal 2 above is met, there's one more place the Godhead doctrine side can retreat to. **"It wasn't two persons conversing — it was the deity and humanity conversing within the one Person."** This answer actually does come up.

**This Rebuttal Is Powerful**: this single move disposes of several problems all at once.

```
the Gethsemane prayer                       → "the humanity to the deity"
the heavenly voice at the baptism           → "the deity to the humanity"
John 12:28, "I will glorify it again"       → "the deity to the humanity"
the entire prayer of John 17                → "the humanity to the deity"
"the Father is greater than I"              → "the humanity, regarding the deity"
```

One explanation blocks five places. That's what makes it troublesome. But this explanation pays a cost in four places.

---

**① An Attribute Cannot Converse**

This is the same problem confirmed in Counter-Rebuttal 2. **To converse, there must be a speaking side and a hearing side, each existing.** But the Godhead doctrine side itself says "a nature is not a person — it is merely an attribute a person possesses."

```
"the deity spoke"    → requires a 'subject' who speaks
"the humanity heard"  → requires a 'subject' who hears
"the humanity petitioned" → requires a 'subject' who asks
```

**An attribute can neither speak nor hear.** The moment one says "the deity spoke to the humanity," the deity and the humanity each become a speaking and hearing subject. That is precisely what they were trying to deny.

And holding this against their own standard again — **conversation uses both intellect and will.**

---

**② "Whether Heaven or Earth" — The Location Doesn't Line Up**

This explanation only holds if it's settled **where the Father is.** But within the Godhead doctrine's own material, that location splits.

```
in one place  → "the soul is in heaven and the body is on earth"
in another    → "the Father present within Christ is the soul"
```

**Does the One present within answer from heaven?** And if the deity is everywhere, why must the sound come specifically **from heaven**? In the baptism scene, the voice clearly comes "from heaven."

---

**③ What Becomes of the Earlier Claim, "There Is No Dialogue Between the Father and the Son"?**

The Godhead doctrine side has said, elsewhere — **"nowhere in Scripture is there dialogue exchanged between the Father and the Son."**

But this rebuttal **grants that dialogue occurred.** It merely claims that what conversed was not two persons but two natures.

**Turning what was said to be "absent" into "present" is not an expansion of the explanation — it's a withdrawal of the earlier statement.** Which is their actual position must be settled first.

---

**④ Decisive — the Humanity Cannot Say Those Words**

This is where this rebuttal actually collapses. Simply look at **the content of the prayer.**

> **John 17:5** — "O Father, glorify thou me with thine own self, with **the glory which I had with thee before the world was**."
>
> **John 17:24** — "for **thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world**."

In the Godhead doctrine's explanation, **the humanity begins in Mary's womb.** Yet the speaker of this prayer says he **had glory and was loved before the world existed.**

> 🚨 **There is something to grant here first.** Simply saying *"the humanity began in the womb, so the humanity cannot say this"* is **not sufficient.** Scripture speaks, regarding the one Christ, of what belongs to the deity and what belongs to the humanity **together** (see §11-18). So the opponent can answer this way — **"the one Christ prayed as humanity, while speaking of the deity's pre-existence."** **This answer must not be left out.**

**So the point at issue is not "which nature's attribute was being spoken."** It is **who, respectively, "I" and "thou" are within the prayer.**

```
If "I" is the humanity
   → the humanity could not have existed or enjoyed glory before the world

If "I" is the deity
   → since the deity is petitioning "thou,"
     "thou" must be distinguished from the speaker

Even if it is "the whole of the one Christ"
   → if "thou," the recipient of the prayer, is the same person as the speaker,
     the structure is still one of petitioning oneself
```

**The difference of natures is a difference of "what something is."** But prayer, love, sending, and answering are relational language of **"who, to whom."** **Speaking of two natures can explain the attributes of the speaker, but it cannot, in its place, explain how the speaker and the recipient are distinguished.**

**⟶ So there is one thing that must be answered.** Who, respectively, are the **"I"** and **"thou"** of John chapter 17? If they are not two, how, **as the text stands**, do you explain the relationship between "the glory I had with thee" and "glorify me"?

---

**⑤ In Addition — What Is Known Differs**

> **Mark 13:32** — "of that day and that hour knoweth no man... **neither the Son, but the Father.**"

between the deity and the humanity, **what is known differs.** If what is known differs, that is **two knowing subjects.** One person cannot simultaneously know and not know the same thing.

---

**⑥ And Intercession Continues Even Now**

> **Heb 7:25** — "he **ever liveth** to make intercession for them"
>
> **1 Tim 2:5** — "there is one God, and one mediator **between God and men**, the man Christ Jesus"

Saying "the humanity to the deity" means that **at this very moment, the humanity is interceding to the deity.** But 1 Timothy 2:5 places the mediator **between God and men** — not between two parts within God.

---

**⟶ So There Is One Question to Ask.**

> **"You said it was the deity speaking to the humanity. Then in John 17:5, the One praying says, 'the glory I had with thee before the world was' — you said the humanity begins in Mary's womb, so how can the humanity speak of glory from before the world existed?**
>
> **Is the speaker of that prayer the deity, or the humanity?"**

**Verdict**: ⚠️ the explanation "the deity and humanity conversed" is powerful in that it disposes of several scenes at once. But ① **the distinction of two natures alone does not explain how the speaker and recipient of the conversation are distinguished**, ② the Father's location shifts from one piece of material to the next, ③ it withdraws the earlier claim "there is no dialogue between the Father and Son," and ④ above all, **it cannot answer who, respectively, "I" and "thou" are in John 17:5.**

> 🚨 **This Item Does Not End With "the Opponent Is Wrong."** Told honestly, the opponent **can answer**, *"the one Christ prayed as humanity while speaking of the deity's pre-existence."* There are no grounds to call that answer itself wrong. **What this item demands is a fixed definition** — if the opponent cannot answer the three questions below **consistently**, the two-natures explanation remains not a rebuttal but a **defensive mechanism.**
>
> ① **"Are the Father and the Son natures, or persons?"**
> ② **"If the humanity is the subject praying, why is the humanity not a person?"**
> ③ **"Who is the 'I' of John 17:5, and how did he enjoy glory with the Father before the Incarnation?"**

---

### Counter-Rebuttal 4: "Sitting at the Right Hand Was 'Until ~,' So It's Temporary"

This is the Godhead doctrine side's primary answer on the right-hand problem.

> **Hebrews 1:13** — "Sit on my right hand, **until** I make thine enemies thy footstool"

The logic runs — *"since there's a set deadline, this is only for a time — it's not because there are two Persons."*

**It is granted that there is a deadline.** "Until ~" is genuinely an expression that has an endpoint. There's no need to dispute this.

**But the question still stands.**

> **"During that period, who is seated at whose right hand?"**

Whether temporary or permanent, **the one seated and the one who commanded the seating are two.** Setting a duration and counting the number of persons are different matters. And the earlier part of the same verse reads, **"sit on my right hand"** — which would make it a picture of the speaker commanding Himself to sit at His own right hand.

**Verdict**: ⚠️ the observation about the deadline is true, but a deadline **does not answer the question of counting persons.**

---

### Counter-Rebuttal 5: "Don't the Souls of Martyrs Also Speak, Separated From the Body?"

This is the Godhead doctrine side's primary answer regarding the baptism scene (the heavenly voice).

> **Revelation 6:9** — "I saw under the altar the **souls** of them that were slain for the word of God"

The logic runs — *"look, the soul speaks and acts even when separated from the body. So it isn't strange that God's soul, separated from the body, spoke from heaven either."*

**The observation is true.** There genuinely is a scene of a martyr's soul speaking.

**But that analogy actually works in the opposite direction.** What does Scripture call it, when spirit and body separate from man?

> **James 2:26** — "**the body without the spirit is dead**"

**The martyr is one who died and was separated.** Substituting that state directly into God means **being separated is the normal state for God.** Then the starting point of this teaching — *"as man is, so is God"* — collapses.

**If separation is death on the man's side but called normal on the God's side, this was never the same kind of analogy to begin with.**

And the baptism scene has **three points** at once.

```
earth (in the water)  → Jesus
above him              → the Holy Spirit descending in the bodily form of a dove
from heaven            → the voice
```

The explanation "the soul spoke to the body" addresses **only two** of those points. It does not explain why the Holy Spirit descended **"upon"** Jesus in a form distinct from Jesus.

**Verdict**: ⚠️ the observation about the martyrs' souls is true, but that example **must be read together with James 2:26, which defines separation as death**, and the third point of the baptism scene still remains.
