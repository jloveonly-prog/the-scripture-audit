# Investigation Document — "the sons of God" Article Usage Investigation

> **Document Role**: This document does not make claims on the "angel/not angel" debate. It is a fact-based investigation into the **English (KJV) and Hebrew article (definite article) usage** for the expression "the sons of God" appearing in Genesis 6:2, 6:4, Job 1:6, 2:1, and 38:7. Interpretations and arguments based on this investigation will be developed separately in the RED/BLUE documents.

## 2. English (KJV) Article Investigation — Fact Check

**All five verses include "the".** Even in Job 38:7, "the" is not omitted; rather, "all" is additionally added before "the sons of God" (structure: "all" + "the sons of God").

- There is no grammatical rule in English that habitually inserts "the" automatically after "all". In the KJV itself, forms like "all + noun without article" are much more commonly used, such as "all flesh" (Isa 40:6), "all nations" (Isa 2:2), and "all things" (Rom 8:28).
- The presence of "the" depends on whether the noun following "all" is a specific/known subject (definite article O) or a general category (definite article X).
- The fact that the translators used "all **the** sons of God" in Job 38:7 is read as a signal that they treated this subject as a specific/known group — they could have written "all sons of God" (no article, general category) but chose not to.

**Summary**: Looking solely at the English text, all 5 verses uniformly use "sons of God with the definite article (the)".

## 3. Hebrew Original Text Article Investigation

| Verse | Hebrew Construction | Definite Article before "Elohim" (ה, ha) |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 6:2, 6:4 | בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים (bene **ha**-Elohim) | Present |
| Job 1:6, 2:1 | בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים (bene **ha**-Elohim) | Present |
| Job 38:7 | כָּל־בְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים (kol-bene Elohim) | **Absent** |

- Genesis 6:2/4 and Job 1:6/2:1 use the **exact same Hebrew construction** (including the definite article, בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים).
- Only in Job 38:7 is "kol" (כָּל, all) attached, and there is no definite article before "Elohim" (בְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים).
- Sources: [BlueLetterBible Original Language Lexicon](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h430/kjv/wlc/0-1/), [StudyLight Interlinear](https://www.studylight.org/interlinear-study-bible/hebrew/job/38-7.html), [BibleHub Interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/job/38-7.htm)

## 4. Was the Absence of the Definite Article Grammatically "Impossible"? — Investigation Result: No

Hebrew grammar does not forbid the construction "kol + ha-X" (all the X) at all. On the contrary, it is a very common pattern throughout the Bible:

- כָּל־הָאָרֶץ (kol ha-arets) = "all the earth" (Gen 11:1, etc.)
- כָּל־הָעָם (kol ha-am) = "all the people" (Exo 19:8, etc.)

In other words, it is not that the author of Job 38:7 could not write "כָּל־בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים" (including the definite article), but rather that he **chose not to write it**. It is a matter of the author's/stylistic choice, not a matter of grammatical coercion/prohibition.

---

## 5. Most Probable Explanation for the Absence of the Definite Article — Prose vs. Poetry Genre Differences

According to Hebrew grammar books (such as Gesenius' Hebrew Grammar §126):

> Hebrew **poetry omits the definite article much more frequently than prose**. Because attaching the definite article "ha" (ה) adds a syllable, it is often omitted for the sake of rhythm/meter, and sometimes for rhetorical reasons. **Even without the definite article, the word can still hold a definite meaning in context.**
> Sources: [Gesenius' Hebrew Grammar §126 (Wikisource)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius'_Hebrew_Grammar/126._Determination_by_Means_of_the_Article), [The Omission of the Definite Article in Biblical Poetry (academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/30145121/The_Omission_of_the_Definite_Article_in_Biblical_Poetry)

**Genre Contrast**:

| Verse | Genre |
|---|---|
| Genesis 6:2, 6:4 | Prose (Narrative) |
| Job 1:6, 2:1 | Prose (Job's prologue narrative framework) |
| Job 38:7 | Poetry — the main body of Job (chapters 3~41) is written in Hebrew poetry |

The fact that the absence of the definite article occurs precisely and solely in the "poetic text" of Job 38:7, while the prose texts of Genesis 6 and the prologue of Job all contain the definite article, is consistent with this explanation.

## 6. Related Investigation — Contrast of "Angel" Vocabulary within Genesis

Apart from "the sons of God" (בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים, bene ha-Elohim), Hebrew has a separate, distinct word pointing to "angel" (messenger): **מַלְאָךְ (mal'akh, H4397)**. This word is used repeatedly within the text of Genesis.

| Verse | Hebrew Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 16:7, 22:11, etc. | מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה (mal'akh YHWH) | "the angel of the LORD" |
| Genesis 21:17 | מַלְאַךְ אֱלֹהִים (mal'akh Elohim) | "the angel of God" (Singular) |
| **Genesis 28:12** | **מַלְאֲכֵי אֱלֹהִים (mal'akhei Elohim)** | **"the angels of God"** (Plural) |
| **Genesis 32:1** | **מַלְאֲכֵי אֱלֹהִים (mal'akhei Elohim)** | **"the angels of God"** (Plural) |

Sources: [BibleStudyTools Gen 28:12 Interlinear](https://www.biblestudytools.com/interlinear-bible/kjv/genesis/28-12.html), [BlueLetterBible Gen 28:12](https://www.blueletterbible.org/tools/interlinear/wlc-kjv/gen/28/12/), [BibleHub Hebrew Concordance — malakhei](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/malachei_4397.htm)

**Investigation Fact**: Genesis 28:12 and 32:1 combine "mal'akh" (angel) + "Elohim" (God) to explicitly write "the angels of God" (mal'akhei Elohim). This is a combinatory expression similar in structure to "bene ha-Elohim" ("sons" + "God") in Genesis 6:2/4, differing only in the first word ("mal'akh" vs "ben/bene"). Thus, the expression "the angels of God" was actually an existing choice available to the author of Genesis.

**Word Characteristic Investigation**: "Mal'akh" (מַלְאָךְ) is a noun indicating a **role (office)** as a "sent one/messenger", whereas "Elohim" (אֱלֹהִים) is a noun indicating a **nature/category** of "divine being/power". The two words have different roots (no common root). "Ben/bene" (בֵּן/בְּנֵי, "son") is a word overwhelmingly used throughout the Bible to refer to lineal/relational birth, but the statistical extent to which this tendency applies to the "bene Elohim" usage was not separately tallied in this investigation — left as an item needing verification.

This fact itself is neutral data and is not interpreted in this document as favoring either "angel" or "not angel".

## 8. Investigation Conclusions (Not Interpretations/Arguments)

1. English KJV Text Level: All 5 verses have "the" — apparently a uniform expression.
2. Hebrew Original Text Level: Gen 6:2/4 and Job 1:6/2:1 have the definite article (same construction), Job 38:7 lacks the definite article — a real difference.

## 9. Related Investigation — Comparison of 4 Vocabularies, and New Testament "Sons of God" / Resurrection-Angel Comparison

### 9-1. At least 4 distinct Hebrew terms refer to heavenly beings and do not overlap

| Expression | Hebrew | Found in Verses |
|---|---|---|
| the sons of God | בְּנֵי (הָ)אֱלֹהִים | Gen 6:2/4, Job 1:6/2:1, Job 38:7 |
| the angel(s) of God | מַלְאַךְ/מַלְאֲכֵי (הָ)אֱלֹהִים | Gen 21:17, 28:12, 32:1, Judg 13:6, etc. |
| the host of heaven | צְבָא הַשָּׁמַיִם (tseva ha-shamayim) | 1 Kings 22:19 |
| a spirit | רוּחַ (ruach) | 1 Kings 22:21 |

**Two Interpretive Possibilities (Cannot choose one, listed in parallel)**:
- **Interpretation A**: Different vocabulary means different referents — "the sons of God" is a unique category distinct from the other expressions.
- **Interpretation B**: Hebrew literature commonly describes the same subject (heavenly spiritual beings) with different words depending on the context/emphasis — they are merely stylistic variations of the same subject, not pointing to different beings.

The grammatical data secured in this investigation alone cannot decide between A and B. Both do not contradict the observations made so far.

### 9-2. The New Testament's "the sons of God" — English expression is identical

| Verse | KJV | Greek Original |
|---|---|---|
| Rom 8:14 | "as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are **the sons of God**" | υἱοὶ (huioi, sons) — **Match** |
| Phil 2:15 | "that ye may be... **the sons of God**, without rebuke" | τέκνα (tekna, children) — **Mismatch** |
| 1 John 3:1-2 | "that we should be called **the sons of God**... now are we **the sons of God**" | τέκνα (tekna, children) — **Mismatch** |

Looking solely at the English expressions, Genesis 6, Job 1, 2, 38, and the New Testament identically use "the sons of God". In the New Testament, this clearly refers to believers (humans) born again by the Holy Spirit. However, this is Greek, and it is based on a redemptive-historical mechanism unique to the New Testament (being born again in the Holy Spirit through faith in Christ) — a mechanism that did not yet exist at the time of Genesis 6 (pre-Christ). The fact that "the English words are the same" is data, but this alone cannot immediately connect "the sons of God" in Genesis 6 to being called so on the same basis as in the New Testament.

**Caution**: As shown in the table above, Phil 2:15 and 1 John 3:1-2 are "teknon" (children, emphasizing lineal relationship) in Greek, but the KJV translated them as "sons". Conversely, in Luke 20:36 (see 9-3), it is "huios" (son, emphasizing legal status) in Greek, but the KJV translated it as "children". **The KJV's choice of English "sons/children" does not consistently reflect the Greek "huios/teknon" distinction.** Therefore, judging this topic solely by English words is unreliable — checking the original language is essential.

### 9-3. At the Resurrection "as the angels" vs "the children of God" — The Bible uses both expressions side by side yet distinguishes them

| Verse | KJV English | Greek Original |
|---|---|---|
| Matt 22:30 | "in the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are **as the angels of God** in heaven" | — |
| **Luke 20:34-36** | "...neither marry, nor are given in marriage: Neither can they die any more: for they are **equal unto the angels**; and are **the children of God**, being the children of the resurrection." | **υἱοί** (huioi, "sons") |

**Important Correction**: In Luke 20:36, the KJV translated it into English as "the children of God", but the Greek original text is **υἱοί (huioi, "sons")** — the **exact same Greek word** as "the sons of God" (υἱοὶ θεοῦ) in Romans 8:14. That is, the KJV English translation merely rendered "sons"/"children" differently depending on the context, but conceptually in the original language, Luke 20:36 uses the same "sonship (huios)" as Romans 8:14.

For reference, Greek has separate words for "son" (υἱός, huios — favored by Paul, emphasizing legal status/adoption: Rom 8:14, 16, 19) and "child" (τέκνον, teknon — favored by John, emphasizing lineal birth/natural relationship: John 1:12, 1 John 3:1-2). **The KJV did not translate these two consistently** (e.g., 1 John 3:1-2 and Phil 2:15 are "teknon" in Greek but KJV translates them as "sons"; Luke 20:36 is "huios" in Greek but KJV translates it as "children"). Therefore, judging this topic "solely by English words (sons/children)" is unreliable, and checking the original language is essential.

**Core Observation (Revised)**: Luke 20:36 describes resurrected beings as "equal unto the angels" (comparison/simile, limiting to specific attributes: no more death, no marriage) and "the children of God" (direct predication, specifying the reason: being the children of the resurrection — Greek huioi), distinguishing the two **within the same sentence**. If being sons of God meant the same thing as being angels, there would be no reason to use both expressions side by side. Furthermore, since this "huioi" is the exact same Greek word as "son" in Romans 8:14, it serves as evidence that the New Testament usage of "son of God" is consistently maintained even within the context of a resurrection-angel comparison.

**Scope Limitation (Important)**: Both Matt 22:30 and Luke 20:34-36 explicitly specify "in the resurrection". This does not refer to an intermediate state where only spirit/soul exists without a body, but to the future event of receiving a resurrected body (1 Cor 15:44 "a spiritual body"). These two verses do not speak directly about the intermediate state — extending them to mean "intermediate state = equal unto the angels" goes beyond the evidentiary scope of these verses.

### 9-4. Adam = "the son of God" (Luke 3:38) — Insufficient evidence whether the title was revoked post-Fall

Luke 3:38 calls Adam "the son of God" without any qualifying clauses in a genealogy recorded long after the Fall. No separate verse has been found stating that this title was revoked after the Fall — rather, the unconditional statement in Luke 3:38 may imply the opposite (maintenance of the title). "Intimate relationship/fellowship with God was severed due to the Fall" (Gen 3:23-24, Rom 5:12) and "the title 'son of God' itself was revoked" are two different claims, and the latter is an unverified inference to date.

### 9-5. "Son" = "Birth" vs "Child" = "Adoption" — New Testament Verification (1 Strong Case + 1 Counterexample)

"Adoption" (legally accepting someone already born) and "birth" (a new nature springing forth like coming from a womb) are different mechanisms. We directly tested whether this distinction aligns with the KJV's choice of the words "son" / "child".

**Strong Case — John 1:12-13 (Same context, immediate next verse)**

| Verse | KJV |
|---|---|
| John 1:12 | "gave he **power** to become the **sons** of God" |
| John 1:13 (immediate next verse) | "Which were **born**, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God." |

Right after mentioning the power (right/qualification) to become "sons," the next verse explains the method as "birth." **Looking solely at this passage, the equation "becoming sons = being born of God (birth)" is clearly established.**

**Supporting Case — Eph 1:5 ("Children" + "Adoption")**

| Verse | KJV |
|---|---|
| Eph 1:5 | "the **adoption** of **children**" |

**Counterexample — Gal 4:5 ("Sons" + "Adoption", exact opposite combination)**

| Verse | KJV |
|---|---|
| Gal 4:5 | "that we might receive the **adoption** of **sons**" |

The same word "sons" is here paired with "adoption" rather than "birth". It directly contradicts John 1:12-13.

**Summary**: The rule "son = birth, child = adoption" holds in John 1:12-13 and Eph 1:5, but breaks in Gal 4:5. It is not an absolute rule across the New Testament, but rather seems to diverge according to the theological emphasis of each author (John = birth/born-again focus, Paul = adoption/legal status focus). **However, John 1:12-13 itself (independent of other verses) establishes a powerful, unshakable equation within its own context: "becoming sons of God by power = being born of God (birth)".**

### 9-6. Is the expression "children/sons of God" also in the Old Testament? — Yes

| Verse | KJV | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Deuteronomy 14:1 | "Ye are the **children** of the LORD your God" | children |
| Hosea 1:10 | "Ye are the **sons** of the living God" (quoted in Rom 9:26) | sons |

**Investigation Fact**: The declaration of the status of "children/sons of God" is not an exclusive New Testament concept but already exists in the Old Testament (Deuteronomy, Hosea). However, these two Old Testament verses do not come with explicit mechanistic explanations of "birth" or "adoption" — they are simply relational declarations of "Ye are...". **Specific descriptive language (mechanisms) such as "birth"/"adoption" appears only in the New Testament within the scope checked so far.**

### 9-7. Inheritance Chain — "A servant does not inherit, only one born of the body/son inherits"

**Genesis 15:2-4**

| Verse | KJV |
|---|---|
| Gen 15:3 (Abram's thought) | "one born in my house is **mine heir**" (Considers servant Eliezer as heir) |
| Gen 15:4 (God's correction) | "**This shall not be thine heir**; but **he that shall come forth out of thine own bowels** shall be thine heir." |

When Abram attempts to make his servant (Eliezer) his heir, God directly corrects him — **A servant is not the heir. Only one born of one's own body is the heir.**

**Hebrews 1:14**

| Verse | KJV |
|---|---|
| Heb 1:14 | "Are they not all **ministering spirits**, sent forth to minister for them **who shall be heirs of salvation**?" |

Angels = "ministering spirits" (servant's role). Humans (those who shall be heirs of salvation) = heirs. **The distinction is explicit: angels are ministers, humans are heirs.**

**Romans 8:17 (Cross-verification)**: "And if **children**, then **heirs**; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ." — Here, "children" is directly linked to inheritance. Thus, the concept of inheritance is not exclusively tied to "son" but applies equally to "children" — consistent with the conclusion in 9-5 that "the word son/child itself is not an absolute rule".

**Synthesis**: There is a consistent flow from Gen 15 (servant ≠ heir) → Gal 4 (no longer a servant but a son when the fulness of the time came = heir) → Heb 1:14 (angel = ministering servant, human = heir) → Rom 8:17 (children = heirs). **The status of "heir" repeatedly appears as the core criterion dividing "servant/angel" and "son/child".**

### 9-8. Wedding Feast Parable — The Bible distinguishes "the bride" and "those called as guests"

| Verse | KJV |
|---|---|
| Matt 11:11 | "there hath not risen a greater than John the Baptist: notwithstanding **he that is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he**" |
| John 3:29 (John the Baptist's self-definition) | "He that hath **the bride** is the bridegroom: but **the friend of the bridegroom**, which standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly" |
| Rev 19:7 | "the marriage of the Lamb is come, and **his wife** hath made herself ready" |
| Rev 19:9 | "Blessed are **they which are called unto** the marriage supper of the Lamb" |

John the Baptist is the greatest figure representing the Old Testament (Matt 11:11), yet he defines himself not as "the bride" but as "**the friend of the bridegroom**" (John 3:29). Rev 19:7-9 grammatically distinguishes "his wife" (the bride, already ready) and "those which are called unto the marriage supper" (guests) within the same paragraph — a bride is not described as being "called" to her own wedding.

**Interpretive Options (Cannot choose one, listed in parallel)**:
- **Option A**: A framework where the bride = the New Testament Church ("sons of God" born again by the blood of Christ), and the friend of the bridegroom/guests = other statuses including John the Baptist (Old Testament saints, etc.).
- **Option B**: This is an interpretation discussed in specific eschatological perspectives (some Independent Baptist/Dispensationalist traditions), and is not a unanimous interpretation across the entire conservative camp.

**Important**: Being a "guest" does not mean being unsaved — John 3:29 says the friend of the bridegroom "rejoiceth greatly", and Rev 19:9 also says they are "Blessed". This is a distinction of **role/status**, not of salvation.

### 9-9. The scope of "we" in Galatians 4 — Interpretive Option (Cannot choose one)

The "we" in Gal 4:1-7 ("we, when we were children, were in bondage") grammatically refers directly to Paul and his contemporary readers (people who experienced the transition from the law to grace within their lifetime). Expanding this to mean "not individual Old Testament saints who already died, but only the generation (descendants) after Christ's coming finally become sons" is a reasonable interpretive option, but the text does not explicitly define it as such — **it is an interpretation recorded, not a confirmed fact.**

### 9-10. "Children of the LORD" (Israel, land inheritance) vs "Children of God" (Universal) — Linguistically weak evidence, but valid theological insight

Deut 14:1 uses "the LORD **your God**" (a combined form of LORD+God, the formal covenantal expression) — since it did not use "LORD" alone or "God" alone, the **linguistic evidence** to clearly separate "children of the LORD" and "children of God" into distinct terms based solely on this verse **is weak.**

However, the core insight that "Israel's childship is linked to the national covenant of land inheritance" aligns well with Deut 14:1 and the inheritance structure of Gen 15. **Therefore, this is recorded not as a linguistic claim regarding "LORD/God word distinction," but as a theological distinction that "Israel's national and land-inheriting childship" and "the New Testament's universal and spiritual childship" are promises of different natures.**

#### 9-13-3-A. ⚠️ "among them" Investigation Result — Not "Pass" but "Ambiguous" (Conducted 2026-07-30)

> **Evidence Hierarchy Compliance**: The order of evidence in this project is ① **KJV Text** ② **Original Language** ③ (For reference) Commentaries. The below is judged solely by ①②, with commentaries appended only as a reference note.

**① KJV Text Level**

> **Job 1:6**: "the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD, and Satan came **also among them**"

- "**also**" is an adverb that **adds Satan separately** from the subject of the previous clause — if he were a member of "the sons of God," there would be no need to mention him "also" separately after stating that the group had already come.
- Conversely, "**among them**" indicates that he was **within the space** of that gathering.
- **By KJV text alone**: It is confirmed that Satan **attended** the gathering, but it is **not confirmed whether he is a member of that group.** "also" marks distinction, "among" marks proximity, and neither determines affiliation.

**② Original Language Level**

The Hebrew **בְּתוֹכָם** (betocham, "among them") **does not necessarily imply affiliation but does not exclude it either.** This prepositional phrase marks spatial position, and membership must be determined separately by the context.

**Judgment Downgrade**: "First test passed" → **⚠️ Ambiguous (Undecided)**. The previous version's statement "syntactically separated so it passes" was an overstatement.
