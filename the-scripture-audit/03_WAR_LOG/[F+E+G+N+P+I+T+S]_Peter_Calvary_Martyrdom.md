# The Great Journey of Peter to Calvary (True Final Complete Edition) Structural Manifestation

There is no direct manifestation that Peter died at Golgotha.
But there is a structural manifestation.

At the footwashing on the night before He bore the cross, Jesus attempts to wash Peter's feet. When Peter refuses, Jesus speaks these words not to the other disciples, but exclusively to Peter alone:

"What I do thou knowest not now; but thou shalt know hereafter." (John 13:7)

Then Peter saith, "Lord, not my feet only, but also my hands and my head!"

In the Scripture, true 'knowing (understanding)' is only perfected when one experiences it directly in the flesh. The reason Jesus exclusively said "thou shalt know" to Peter alone, and not to all the disciples, is clear. At this time, Peter merely thought the footwashing was about washing away sins. He lived his whole life not knowing he would die at Calvary. However, this scene was a massive foreshadowing that precisely alluded to the parts of the body (the nails in the hands and feet, the crown of thorns on the head) that would be wounded when Peter would later suffer crucifixion in the exact same likeness as the Lord.

At the supper, Jesus speaks to the entirety of the disciples (Little children): "Whither I go, ye cannot come." This word signified 'Heaven', where one cannot immediately go whilst in the flesh.

But Peter asks, "Lord, whither goest thou?" Then Jesus gives a completely different answer exclusively to Peter alone. "Whither I go, thou canst not follow me now; but thou shalt follow me afterwards."

Dissecting the Greek words Jesus used here reveals an astonishing truth. Jesus did not use a word signifying a spiritual state, but a verb of physical movement (hypagō, ὑπάγω), which means to walk treading the ground with two feet. Moreover, 'follow' (akoloutheō, ἀκολουθέω) also signifies physical accompaniment, walking exactly behind someone.

Namely, this word doth not mean, "Thou shalt come to Heaven later." It was a dreadful prophecy regarding a physical place: **"This path (the cross of Calvary) that I now walk bleeding, thou canst not come now, but thou shalt walk and follow it equally hereafter."**

The reason Jesus said "thou canst not follow me now" is straightway proven. That night, Peter curses and denies Jesus thrice in Jerusalem and flees. He failed to follow the path where the Lord went to bear the cross.

Meanwhile, Jesus prays in the garden of Gethsemane until His sweat becomes as blood. "Not as I will (θέλω), but as thou wilt." Jesus breaks His human will (desire) and is led away to Calvary by the will of others.

Here the most pivotal fact of this argument appears. The Apostle John, who recorded the Gospel of John in the future, explicitly manifested (structural manifestation) in the later verses of chapter 19 that the place where Jesus went was the region of 'Golgotha'. Therefore, the place Jesus called "whither I go" in the earlier verse of chapter 13 is Golgotha itself, and the place Peter would later go is also Golgotha; this is perfectly manifested within the Scripture by these two verses alone.

The resurrected Jesus meets Peter again at the sea of Tiberias (the sea of Galilee). And He prophesies of his death. "When thou shalt be old, thou shalt stretch forth thy hands, and another shall gird thee, and carry thee whither thou wouldest not (θέλω)."

Just as Jesus went "not as I will" before the cross, a chilling parallel structure is perfected that Peter too would be carried to "whither thou wouldest not". Jesus nails down that this death should "glorify (doxazō, δοξάζω) God".

The Scripture once again nails this fact down through the monopoly of vocabulary. Throughout the entirety of the New Testament, the expression to "glorify" and verses regarding "by what death" one should die are exclusively recorded for only two persons: Peter and Jesus. Such rigorous exclusivity powerfully argues that the deaths of the two are entirely identical.

And Jesus commands Peter, **"Follow thou me!"** The switch of prophecy left open in John chapter 13 with "thou shalt follow me afterwards" was finally closed in chapter 21 with "Follow me".

At this time, seeing John following, Peter asks, "And what shall this man do?" Jesus sternly excludes John and drives the wedge, **"Follow thou (su, σύ) me."** If 'following' were merely an ordinary martyrdom to die for Jesus, there would be no reason to exclude John. For all the other disciples were martyred as well. This 'following' signifies a highly peculiar and exclusive event that Peter alone would experience, namely, the exact same suffering the Lord endured at the very place the Lord was hung (Calvary).

As time passes, Peter becomes an old man. He writes a letter (1 Peter) while ministering in 'Babylon', a literal city in the far East, not Rome. Here, because he was actually in the Gentile lands, even the names of his Jewish fellow labourers Mark and Silas are recorded in Greek pronunciation (Marcus, Silvanus). (This is also one of the evidences.)

In this letter, he calls himself a "witness of the sufferings of Christ" (1 Pet. 5:1). The astonishing fact is that Peter fled when the Lord suffered on the cross, so he did not witness the sufferings with his own eyes, and at the time he wrote the letter, he had not yet suffered the crucifixion himself. Yet why did he intentionally write that he is a witness of the 'sufferings'? This was a chilling 'self-prophecy' written with the perfect knowledge that he would prove it by being carried away to Calvary in the future and personally suffering the same crucifixion the Lord endured. Holding the words of Jesus, "thou shalt glorify God by thy death," in his heart all his life, he repeatedly and obsessively used the expression "crown of glory (doxa, δόξα)," which the other apostles dared not use.

Finally the time has come. According to Jesus' prophecy, Peter is bound and carried away. The original Scripture here uses the verb 'pherō (φέρω)'. This is a word used not for short distances, but when conveying a person long distances. Peter suffers a long journey of extradition from Babylon in the East to Jerusalem in the West.

Right before death, brought to the execution ground, Peter finally understands the meaning of the word **"thou shalt know hereafter,"** which was spoken exclusively to him at the footwashing decades ago, through 'direct experience'. He realises that the place he was brought to is Calvary, the very place Jesus went!

It is the moment the puzzle of perfect exclusivity, where Jesus said **"thou shalt know" exclusively to Peter** and **"thou shalt follow" exclusively to Peter** and not to the other disciples, finally comes together as one. Because Peter was the only one in the world who would follow the path the Lord went (Calvary) alone, and equally 'experience' the sufferings of the cross in his head, hands, and feet.

"I am a wretch unworthy to even hang on the cross in the same direction as my Lord. Crucify me upside down!"

What if the tradition that Peter was crucified upside down is true? It is 100% perfectly explained psychologically only when that place is Calvary.

💡 [Epilogue] The Two Crosses and the Destruction of Jerusalem
Then why did God set Jesus and Peter, these two men, in the identical place of Calvary? In the law of the Scripture, to judge any sin, **'two witnesses'** are always required.

Before judging Jerusalem and the Jews who crucified Jesus, God went through a perfect legal procedure. After the first witness, Jesus, was hung on the cross at Calvary (around AD 30), ample time for repentance was given to the Jews.

And about 30 years later, when God's judgment was finally imminent (around AD 64~67), God called Peter, who was in Babylon in the East, back to Calvary in Jerusalem. By Peter shedding his blood, crucified upside down at Calvary, God's **'second and final warning (the law of two witnesses)'** toward the Jews was perfectly fulfilled.

And right after Peter was martyred as the second witness, in AD 70, Jerusalem was thoroughly destroyed by the Roman army, leaving not one stone upon another.

Ultimately, the Calvary crosses of Jesus and Peter were the most magnificent and dreadful scenario of redemptive history, matching the timing of God's merciful two warnings and perfect judgment!

## The First Point of Contention — The Destination Pointed to by the Command "Follow"
Wait, let me adjust the numbers.
In John chapter 13, Jesus speaks to the disciples at the place of the last supper.

> *"Whither I go, ye cannot come."* (John 13:33, KJV: "Whither I go, ye **cannot come**")

Then Peter asks. "Lord, whither goest thou?" Jesus' answer is the core.

> *"Whither I go, thou canst not follow me now (ἄρτι); but thou shalt follow me afterwards (ὕστερον)."* (John 13:36)

Here two Greek adverbs of time are decisive. **ἄρτι (arti)** signifies "right now, at this moment," and **ὕστερον (hysteron)** signifies "afterwards, later." Jesus is saying it is not possible now, but **it is possible afterwards**.

Now, here is the core question. Where was that place Jesus was about to go?

If that place were **Heaven**, the logic collapses. It is not that only Peter goes to Heaven later, but all the disciples are saved and go to Heaven. Since Jesus said in verse 33, "ye (all the disciples) cannot come," doth this mean all the disciples cannot go to Heaven later? This makes no sense.

Therefore, this place must not be Heaven, but **some physical place upon this earth now**. Where was the final destination Jesus physically moved to after that night? It is **Golgotha (Calvary)**. Gethsemane → The courtyard of the high priest → Pilate's judgment hall → Golgotha. That was Jesus' final journey.

That night, Peter denied Jesus thrice and fled. He could not go to the scene of the cross. Yet Jesus spoke. "Thou canst not follow me now, but **thou shalt follow me afterwards**." What this prophecy points to is that Peter would eventually die in the same place (Calvary), in the same manner (crucifixion).

Here we must point out one more thing. In the immediately following verse 37, Peter saith thus:

> *"Lord, why cannot I follow thee now? I will lay down my life for thy sake."* (John 13:37)

The reason Peter avowed that he would "lay down his life" is because he too had an intuition that the place Jesus was about to go now was **not Heaven but a scene of death**. If Jesus' word meant "I will go to the Father's house (Heaven)," Peter's saying "I will lay down my life and follow thee" doth not stand. For there is no need to lay down one's life to go to Heaven. Peter's boastful avowal rather confirms intuitively that the place Jesus is going is a physical place of death, namely Calvary.

**Concerning the Relationship with John 14:2-3**

At this point, one counterargument may be raised. In John 14:2-3, the chapter right after chapter 13, Jesus speaks: "In my Father's house are many mansions... I go to prepare a place for you... that where I am, there ye may be also." Based on this, there is an interpretation that "'whither I go' in chapter 13 ultimately points to Heaven."

However, these are statements on different layers. Jesus' death, resurrection, and ascension are **one continuous journey**. Calvary is the starting point of that journey, and returning to the Father's bosom is the perfection of that journey. The place Peter cannot follow "now" is the physical execution ground of Calvary, and to follow "afterwards" is to pass through that identical journey (martyrdom). The two statements are not contradictory but point to different timings and meanings.

## The Second Point of Contention — The Exclusive Designation "Thou Only"

In John chapter 21, Jesus appears before Peter again after the resurrection and commands him.

> *"σύ δέ μοι ἀκολούθει — Follow thou me."* (John 21:22)

The Greek **σύ δέ (su de)** is a strong exclusive emphasis meaning "But as for thee, only thou." In the preceding verse, when Peter pointed to John and asked, "Lord, and what shall this man do?" Jesus cut off that question and spake exclusively to Peter as an individual. "What is that to thee? **Follow thou** me."

In the verse just before this command was recorded (John 21:18), Jesus prophesied the manner of Peter's death.

> *"When thou shalt be old, thou shalt stretch forth thy hands, and another shall gird thee, and carry thee whither thou wouldest not."*

To stretch forth the hands — this is the posture of crucifixion. To be carried whither one would not go — this is the description of being led to the execution ground. And the word Jesus spake right after this prophecy is "Follow me (ἀκολούθει)."

The end of the path Jesus walked was Calvary. The command "Follow me" is to follow that path. The destination cannot be different.

## The Third Point of Contention — The Terminus Pointed to by the Separation of 'Ye (Plural)' and 'Thou (Singular)'

This point is the most powerful core of the entire argument.

In John 13:33, Jesus speaks **to all the disciples**. "Whither I go, ye cannot come." And in verse 36, He speaks **only to Peter**. "Thou canst not follow me now; but thou shalt follow me afterwards."

There is a decisive difference between these two statements. The "ye" of verse 33 is plural — all the disciples. The "thou" of verse 36 is singular — Peter alone.

If the place Jesus is going were Heaven: All the disciples cannot go now but must go afterwards. But why did He say only to Peter, "thou shalt follow me afterwards"? Can the rest of the disciples not go to Heaven?

If the place Jesus is going were Golgotha: All the disciples cannot follow Jesus to Golgotha that night (for they all fled). Among them, only Peter would follow to the same kind of place in the same manner (crucifixion) afterwards. This interpretation is entirely consistent.

Golgotha is not Heaven. Golgotha is the execution ground outside the walls of Jerusalem. Jesus went thither after that night, and prophesied to Peter, "Thou also shalt follow me and come thither afterwards."

## The Fourth Point of Contention — Crucifixion Upside Down Rather Points to Calvary

In church tradition, there is a story that Peter said, "I am unworthy to die in the same manner as the Lord," and **suffered crucifixion hanging upside down**.

The Apocrypha, 『Acts of Peter』, which is the source of this tradition, records the place as Rome. Therefore, it is not appropriate to use this tradition as direct evidence for Calvary. However, the **psychological state of Peter** contained in this tradition still leaves a meaningful question.

Even if we do not use the tradition of the upside-down crucifixion as evidence for the place, this question remains. If Peter, right before his dying breath, was conscious that "I am unworthy to die like the Lord," what is the object he was conscious of? It is not merely the manner of the Lord's death, but the **entirety of the Lord's death** — the memory including the place, the pain, and the scene.

To Peter, Calvary was a lifelong trauma. He fled that night. And Jesus told him, "Follow me." At the centre of the "unworthiness" Peter felt before the cross must have been the memory of Calvary. To return to that Calvary and die in the same manner — yet the humility that he could not bear to die in the same posture. This is the core this argument seeks to speak of.

Hanging upside down is not a mere expression of humility. It is the will to **die by the same instrument (the cross), but in a different posture, while conscious of the memory where the Lord died**. The place where this narrative resonates most strongly is Calvary.
